## Day 53 — The Lion and the Trap

### Abba John the Dwarf

Abba John asked who could be stronger than a lion. Yet even the lion can be captured when its appetite draws it toward what has been placed in the trap. Its great strength cannot protect it when desire directs its movement.

John’s point is not that appetite itself is evil. Hunger belongs to the creature, just as many other desires belong naturally to human life. The danger comes when desire becomes powerful enough to lead us without discernment.

### Devotional

We often imagine that strength means being able to resist temptation through force of will. John offers a more complicated picture. The lion is immensely strong, yet strength alone does not keep it free.

The trap works because something desirable is placed inside it. The lion does not walk toward captivity because it wants to be captured. It moves toward something it wants and discovers too late that the desire has carried it somewhere else.

Much of human desire works this way. We rarely choose what harms us because we consciously want the harm itself. We want relief, pleasure, comfort, recognition, excitement, security, or escape. The difficulty is that the thing promising one of these goods may also carry consequences we were not attending to.

This makes curiosity about desire important. When an urge appears, we can ask what it is actually promising us. Perhaps we are hungry for rest but reach for stimulation, lonely but seek distraction, anxious but pursue control, or discouraged and look for something that will briefly change how we feel. The visible desire may be carrying a deeper hunger underneath it.

John’s lion also reminds us that desire narrows attention. Once the bait becomes the center of awareness, the trap surrounding it becomes harder to see. The mind begins emphasizing the immediate reward while minimizing what may follow. What appears obvious from a distance can become strangely difficult to perceive from inside the wanting.

This is why discernment often needs to happen before the moment of strongest desire. We can learn the shape of our traps when we are calm enough to see them clearly. We can notice the situations, emotions, environments, and thoughts that repeatedly pull us toward choices we later regret. Awareness does not eliminate desire, but it changes how blindly we must follow it.

There is no shame in having an appetite. The lion is not weak because it becomes hungry, and human beings are not defective because they want comfort, pleasure, connection, or relief. The spiritual task is not to become creatures without desire. It is to become increasingly free in how we respond to what we desire.

That freedom includes learning to wait. An urge can feel as though it requires immediate action, yet many desires change when they are allowed to remain present without being satisfied at once. We can notice the wanting, feel it in the body, and allow some space around it. The presence of desire does not require obedience to desire.

Sometimes the space reveals something important. We may discover that what seemed like a need was only a passing impulse, or that another response could address the deeper hunger more honestly. We may also discover that the desire points toward something genuinely needed. Discernment helps us distinguish between the bait and the hunger beneath it.

John’s image therefore calls for something wiser than simply becoming stronger. The lion already has strength. What it lacks in the moment is awareness of the relationship between what attracts it and where that attraction leads. Freedom grows when desire is joined to attention.

Over time, we can learn to recognize the familiar path toward the trap. We notice the first movement, the story the mind begins telling, and the promise attached to the thing we want. Then we have a little more room to choose what happens next. That small space may be where freedom begins.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Help me see what I truly seek.*

**Breathing out:** *Lead my desire toward freedom.*
