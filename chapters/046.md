## Day 46 — The Light Is Not Diminished

### Abba John the Dwarf

An elder came to Abba John because he struggled with forgetfulness. John gave him a word of guidance, but by the time the elder returned to his cell, he had forgotten it and had to come back. This happened repeatedly until the elder finally became embarrassed and told John that he had stopped returning because he did not want to burden him.

John asked him to light a lamp and then bring several more lamps to be lit from the first. When the elder had done so, John asked whether the original flame had lost anything by giving its light to the others. The elder saw that it had not, and John told him that he too was not diminished when others came to receive from him.

### Devotional

Many of us carry an instinct to protect what we have because we fear it may run out. We guard our time, patience, attention, energy, and emotional capacity because each one feels limited. There is wisdom in recognizing those limits, yet fear can sometimes convince us that every act of giving leaves us with less.

John offers a different image. One flame can light another without becoming smaller. The second lamp does not steal fire from the first, and the room becomes brighter because the flame was shared. John sees something similar happening when what has been given to us becomes nourishment for another person.

The elder’s forgetfulness makes the story especially tender. He is not asking John for something new each time. He keeps needing the same thing because he cannot hold onto what he has received. Eventually his repeated need becomes embarrassing to him.

Many people know that feeling. We may need reassurance about the same fear, help with the same struggle, or patience around the same weakness. We can begin to imagine that our repetition makes us difficult to love. Shame tells us that we should have learned the lesson already and that asking again will exhaust the people who care for us.

John does not respond that way. He does not make the elder feel foolish for forgetting or calculate how many times he has already explained the same thing. He recognizes that patience can itself be a form of spiritual generosity. His willingness to remain available becomes part of the healing the elder needs.

This does not mean that human beings have infinite energy. There are real limits to what any one person can carry, and healthy love sometimes requires rest, boundaries, or shared responsibility. John’s image is not an argument for exhausting ourselves. It is an invitation to notice that generosity does not always operate according to the arithmetic of scarcity.

Some things increase when they are shared. Encouragement can deepen our own gratitude, teaching can clarify what we ourselves believe, and mercy offered to another can make us more aware of the mercy sustaining us. A patient response can strengthen both the person who receives it and the one who practices it. Love often circulates rather than simply leaving us.

This is especially true when what we give has first been received. John does not present himself as the source of the light. His life is rooted in the love of Christ, and that deeper source allows him to welcome those who come without imagining that every request draws from a private reserve. The flame he shares did not begin with him.

That distinction can change how we approach service. If we believe everything depends on our own strength, every need can feel threatening. We begin measuring how much we have left and worrying about whether another person will take too much. When we remember that we too are continually receiving, generosity can become less defensive.

The story also asks us to reconsider repetition. Growth rarely happens because we hear the right thing once and never need to hear it again. We forget, become distracted, return to old patterns, remember again, and slowly allow truth to sink deeper. Patient love makes room for that process.

Perhaps we can offer others the patience we ourselves require. Someone may need to hear again that they are welcome, forgiven, capable, loved, or not alone. Their need for repetition does not necessarily mean nothing is changing. Sometimes the light simply needs to be kindled again.

John reminds us that sharing light does not mean extinguishing ourselves. When love is grounded in something deeper than our need to be sufficient, giving can become participation rather than depletion. We receive light, we pass it on, and the room grows brighter.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Let me receive your light.*

**Breathing out:** *Let me share it freely.*
