## Day 97 — The Heart That Never Stops Turning

### Abba Sisoes

As Abba Sisoes lay near death, several elders gathered around him. They noticed that he seemed to be speaking with someone they could not see, and they asked what was happening. Sisoes explained that he saw beings approaching to take him away, and that he was asking them to leave him a little longer so he might repent. Even after a lifetime devoted to prayer and spiritual discipline, his desire was for another opportunity to turn toward God.

One of the elders asked what good a little more time could accomplish. Sisoes was weak, and his life was nearly finished, so there seemed little opportunity for further spiritual work. But Sisoes replied that even if he could do nothing else, he could still offer a sigh of sorrow for his soul, and that would be enough. His final concern was not to defend his accomplishments, but to remain open to the transforming work of repentance.

### Devotional

We often think of repentance as something that belongs to the beginning of the spiritual journey. We recognize our mistakes, turn away from destructive habits, and begin pursuing a different way of life. Over time, we hope to become the kind of people who no longer need to revisit those early struggles. Repentance seems like a stage we should eventually leave behind.

Sisoes offers a different understanding. After years of prayer, solitude, and disciplined attention, he still longs for the opportunity to repent. His desire may seem strange to those who imagine spiritual maturity as freedom from every fault. Yet perhaps he has discovered that repentance is not merely a response to wrongdoing, but an ongoing openness to transformation.

There is a difference between believing that we are perpetually inadequate and recognizing that we are perpetually capable of growth. The first can lead to discouragement, shame, and an exhausting preoccupation with our failures. The second allows us to remain curious about the places where love has not yet fully taken root. We can acknowledge our incompleteness without interpreting it as evidence that our lives have gone wrong.

The desert tradition often describes repentance through tears, sorrow, and the remembrance of death. Such language can seem severe, especially when we have experienced religion as a constant reminder of our unworthiness. But sorrow need not be the same as self-condemnation. Sometimes it is simply the tenderness that arises when we recognize how our actions have wounded ourselves or others.

Consider the difference between regretting a harsh word and condemning ourselves for having spoken it. Regret may open us to understanding the pain we caused and encourage us to respond differently in the future. Self-condemnation, however, can turn our attention inward until we become more concerned with our failure than with the person we have hurt. Genuine repentance moves us toward repair rather than trapping us in an endless examination of our own inadequacy.

The most striking part of Sisoes's story may be his willingness to offer something so small. He does not ask for another decade in which to accomplish great spiritual works or demonstrate extraordinary discipline. He asks only for enough time to offer a sigh. In that modest gesture, we encounter the possibility that transformation begins not with impressive achievements, but with the simple movement of a willing heart.

This is an important reminder when we feel overwhelmed by the distance between our intentions and our actual lives. We may see patterns that have persisted for years, relationships that remain wounded, or habits we have repeatedly promised to change. The work of healing can appear so immense that we hesitate to begin. Yet even a small movement toward honesty, compassion, or reconciliation has meaning.

Sisoes invites us to remain open to growth without making our incompleteness a source of despair. We do not need to reach a point where we can confidently declare that nothing remains to be healed. Nor must we measure the sincerity of our repentance by the intensity of our sorrow. As long as the heart remains willing to turn toward love, the spiritual journey remains alive within us.
