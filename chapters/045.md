# Day 45 — More Than One Faithful Way

### Abba Arsenius and Abba Moses

A brother came hoping to visit Abba Arsenius and was taken to his cell. Arsenius received him quietly, and they sat together without conversation. The visitor soon became uncomfortable and asked instead to be taken to Abba Moses.

Moses welcomed the visitors warmly and received them with joy. Afterward, the brother admitted that he preferred Moses’ way of hospitality to Arsenius’ silence. A father who heard this prayed for understanding, and in a vision he saw Arsenius sailing peacefully with the Spirit of God while Moses traveled with angels, sharing sweetness together.

The vision did not declare one way superior to the other. Arsenius withdrew from people for God’s sake, while Moses welcomed people for the same reason. Their outward practices differed, but both could become expressions of faithfulness.

### Devotional

We are often tempted to turn our spiritual temperament into a rule for everyone else. If silence nourishes us, we may suspect that people who enjoy conversation are avoiding depth. If hospitality comes naturally, we may interpret solitude as coldness or withdrawal.

The visitor in this story makes an understandable judgment. Moses feels warmer, easier, and more immediately welcoming, while Arsenius leaves him uncomfortable. From the visitor’s perspective, one experience simply feels more loving than the other.

The vision widens that perspective. Arsenius’ silence is not shown as rejection, nor is Moses’ hospitality portrayed as distraction. Each man has found a way of ordering his life toward God that fits the work given to him. Their faithfulness takes different forms because their callings are not identical.

This matters whenever we compare spiritual lives. One person may encounter God most deeply in silence, another in service, another in study, another in community, and another through creative work. These differences do not automatically indicate different levels of devotion. They may simply reveal different ways that love has taken shape.

Comparison becomes dangerous when we assume that what is fruitful for us must be universal. We can easily mistake temperament for virtue. The introvert may call withdrawal contemplation, while the extrovert calls constant activity hospitality. Both can be faithful, and both can also hide from what they most need.

Discernment therefore requires more than looking at the outward practice. The deeper question is what the practice is producing in us. Does solitude make us more available to love when we return to others, or does it become a refuge from relationship? Does hospitality open us to another person, or does it become a way to avoid silence and ourselves?

The story does not remove the need for self-examination. Arsenius and Moses are not justified merely because they have different personalities. Their ways have been shaped into disciplines directed toward God. What matters is that each practice has become a place of surrender rather than self-protection.

This can free us from unnecessary imitation. We do not have to reproduce another person’s prayer life, personality, or pattern of service in order to be faithful. We can learn from them without becoming them. The task is to discover what form of attention allows love to become most truthful in our own life.

It can also make us more generous toward people whose path looks unfamiliar. Someone else’s silence may carry a depth we cannot see, and someone else’s warmth may contain a generosity that would exhaust us. We do not need to rank those differences in order to appreciate them. The same Spirit can move through very different lives.

Hospitality itself can therefore take more than one form. Moses welcomes through conversation and shared joy, while Arsenius offers the integrity of a life rooted in stillness. One opens the door outward, and the other protects an interior space where prayer can deepen. Both can become gifts when they arise from love rather than fear.

The challenge is to remain faithful to our own calling without making it the measure of everyone else. We can honor the grace given to another person without needing their path to resemble ours. When we stop ranking the forms love takes, we may begin to recognize how much larger the work of God is than our preferred expression of it.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me the way you have given me.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to honor another’s path.*
