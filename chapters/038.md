## Day 38 — Look Closer to Home

### Abba Poemen and Abba Dioscoros

A brother told Abba Poemen that his thoughts kept pulling his attention away from his own sins and toward the faults of another person. Poemen answered by telling him about Abba Dioscoros, who would sit alone in his cell and weep over his own failures. When his disciple protested that Dioscoros seemed to have little to weep about, the old man replied that if he could truly see all his own sins, several people would not be enough to weep for them.

Dioscoros was not teaching self-hatred. He was describing the depth of self-knowledge that makes condemnation difficult. The more honestly he looked within, the less energy remained for standing above another person.

### Devotional

There is something strangely attractive about another person’s fault. Their failure gives our attention somewhere to go that feels both urgent and safe. As long as we are examining them, we do not have to look very closely at ourselves.

Poemen recognizes this movement immediately.

The brother does not say that he has no sins of his own. He says that his thoughts keep setting them aside while becoming preoccupied with someone else’s. Judgment becomes a kind of distraction.

We know this pattern well.

Another person behaves badly, and suddenly our mind becomes remarkably focused. We analyze what they said, what they should have done, why they probably did it, and what their behavior reveals about their character. Meanwhile, our own unfinished places quietly move out of view.

Dioscoros turns the gaze back home.

His language about weeping can sound severe to modern ears, especially if we associate repentance with shame or self-punishment. But the Desert Fathers often used tears as a sign that the heart had become soft enough to face truth. Tears meant that a person no longer needed to hide.

There is a great difference between shame and honest sorrow.

Shame says, *There is something fundamentally wrong with me.* Repentance says, *There are things in me that still need healing.* One closes the heart, while the other keeps it open.

Dioscoros does not claim to be worse than everyone else.

He simply knows that his own interior life is deep enough to occupy him. There are motives he does not fully understand, wounds that still shape his reactions, habits that return when he is tired, and places where love remains incomplete. His own soul is already a vast field of work.

That realization changes how we see other people.

When we remember how complicated our own transformation has been, another person’s failure becomes less surprising. We may still recognize harm clearly, but superiority becomes harder to sustain. Their weakness begins to look less like evidence that they are fundamentally different from us.

Self-knowledge can therefore become a form of mercy.

It reminds us how often we have needed patience, how slowly some patterns change, and how much grace has been required simply to bring us this far. We remember the distance between knowing what is right and consistently doing it.

None of this requires ignoring another person’s behavior.

There are times when harm must be confronted, boundaries established, or injustice named. But we can do those things without imagining ourselves morally untouched by the same human condition. We can stand against what is wrong without standing above the person.

Perhaps this is what Poemen wants the troubled brother to discover.

When your attention becomes fascinated with another person’s faults, come home. Look gently but honestly at what is still unfinished within you, not so that you can condemn yourself, but so that you can remember your dependence on mercy.

The strange thing is that this inward turn can make us more compassionate rather than less.

We become slower to reduce another person to their failure because we know how much more there is to our own story. We become less interested in verdicts and more interested in healing. And the mercy we discover we need becomes the mercy we are increasingly able to offer.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me my own heart.*

**Breathing out:** *Make me gentle with another.*
