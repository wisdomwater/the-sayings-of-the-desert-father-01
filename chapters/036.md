## Day 36 — Who Am I to Judge?

### Abba Joseph of Panephysis

Abba Poemen asked Abba Joseph how one becomes a monk. Joseph told him that if he wanted to find rest in this life and the next, he should meet every circumstance with a simple question: *Who am I?* He joined that question with a second practice—do not judge another person.

Joseph places peace surprisingly close to humility. Rest does not come from understanding everyone around us or reaching the correct verdict about their lives. It comes from remembering who we are before we decide who they are.

### Devotional

The question *Who am I?* can be heard in many ways. It might sound philosophical, inviting us to explore identity, purpose, personality, or vocation. Joseph uses it more like a doorway back into proportion.

Before judging another person, remember yourself.

Remember your own unfinished places, your contradictions, your hidden motives, your good intentions that did not always become good actions. Remember how much of your own life even you do not fully understand. That memory can make certainty about someone else feel less natural.

Judgment often begins by forgetting this.

We watch another person make a decision and imagine that their motives are obvious. We see the behavior but not the fear beneath it, the history behind it, the pressure surrounding it, or the interior struggle that preceded it. Then we construct the missing pieces ourselves.

Soon we are judging a story partly written by our imagination.

Joseph offers a remarkably simple interruption. *Who am I?* The question does not mean that our perceptions are always wrong, but it reminds us that we are not standing outside the human condition looking down upon it.

We are inside it too.

We know what it is to misjudge, rationalize, become defensive, fail under pressure, and understand something only after the moment has passed. We know what it is to need patience while still becoming someone different. Remembering this does not erase discernment; it softens the posture from which discernment happens.

That may be why Joseph connects non-judgment with rest.

Judgment is exhausting because it asks us to maintain an inner courtroom. We gather evidence, replay conversations, assign motives, prepare arguments, and revisit the verdict. The other person may be nowhere near us, yet we remain mentally entangled with them.

Humility closes the courtroom.

We may still need to decide what behavior we will accept or how close a relationship can safely remain. We may need to name something as dishonest, harmful, or unjust. But we do not have to turn that discernment into a complete judgment of the person.

There is a great difference between saying *this action was wrong* and saying *I now know what you are.*

The first may sometimes be necessary. The second claims knowledge we rarely possess. A human life is too deep, unfinished, and hidden for such easy conclusions.

Joseph's question returns us to our own ground.

*Who am I?* I am someone still learning. I am someone capable of both generosity and selfishness, clarity and confusion, courage and fear.

And I am someone who lives by mercy.

Once we remember that, another person's weakness begins to look less alien. We may still disagree, grieve, confront, or step away, but contempt loses some of its power. The person before us becomes a fellow traveler rather than a case to be decided.

Perhaps that is the rest Joseph describes.

We give back the work of final judgment because it was never ours to carry. We attend to our own heart, our own actions, and the love required of us in this moment. The burden becomes lighter when we remember both who we are and who we are not.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Who am I before you?*

**Breathing out:** *Release me from judging another.*
