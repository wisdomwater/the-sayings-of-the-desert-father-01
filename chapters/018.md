## Day 18 — The Same Work

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen once spoke of three people whose lives looked very different from one another. One remained in stillness and practiced solitude faithfully, another was sick yet received his condition with gratitude, and the third spent his days serving others with a pure heart.

From the outside, they appeared to be doing three different things. One was quiet, one was suffering, and one was active. Yet Poemen said that all three were engaged in the same work.

The form of their lives was different, but the movement of the heart was the same. Each was learning to receive the life actually given to him and to meet it with faithfulness.

### Devotional

We often associate the contemplative life with a particular setting. We imagine silence, solitude, prayer, perhaps a quiet room or a retreat far from ordinary demands. These things can be deeply valuable, but Poemen refuses to let contemplation belong only to the person who has managed to become still.

He places beside the solitary a sick person who cannot choose his circumstances and a servant whose attention is constantly turned toward the needs of others. Their lives could hardly look more different, yet Poemen says they are doing the same work. Something deeper than outward activity must therefore define the spiritual life.

Perhaps the common thread is consent.

The solitary consents to remain still instead of escaping into distraction. The sick person consents to inhabit a body and situation that may not be what he would have chosen. The servant consents to give attention to another person rather than making every moment revolve around himself.

None of these forms is automatically holy. Solitude can become avoidance, sickness can fill us with bitterness, and service can become a way of seeking approval or controlling others. What matters to Poemen is the interior posture within the circumstance.

This can be liberating for those of us who imagine that spiritual depth requires arranging life correctly first. We may tell ourselves that we will become more contemplative when work becomes less demanding, when the family needs less from us, when the body feels better, or when we finally have enough quiet. Poemen suggests that the work can already be happening here.

Stillness may sometimes look like sitting alone in prayer. At other times it may look like remaining inwardly present while the body is weak, plans have been interrupted, and nothing about the day resembles what we wanted. There can be profound contemplation in simply refusing to turn suffering into resentment.

And sometimes stillness takes the form of service. A person may spend the day cooking, cleaning, listening, caring, repairing, answering questions, or tending someone who cannot repay them. If the heart is present and uncluttered by the need to be admired, Poemen sees no lesser path there.

This changes the question we bring to our spiritual lives. Instead of asking, *How do I create the perfect conditions for prayer?* we might ask, *What is faithfulness asking of me in the conditions already here?* The answer may still include seeking solitude, but it may also include accepting interruption, limitation, or responsibility without believing that God has somehow become inaccessible.

The deepest work may not depend on what our day looks like from the outside. Two people can perform the same task while inhabiting completely different interior worlds, and two people living completely different lives may be undergoing the same transformation. The desert is not ultimately a geography.

It is the place where we learn to meet reality without continually fleeing from it.

Poemen gives us permission to stop comparing forms. The quiet person, the suffering person, and the serving person may all be walking toward the same center. What matters is whether the heart is becoming present, grateful, and free enough to receive God in the life that is actually here.

### Breath Prayer
