## Day 4 — Strength for the Struggle

### Abba John the Dwarf

Abba Poemen told a story about John the Dwarf, who had grown weary of the passions that troubled him. John prayed that God would take them away, and in time the inner conflict became quiet. The thoughts and desires that had pressed against him seemed to lose their strength, and John found himself living in an unfamiliar peace.

He went to an elder and told him what had happened. “I have no battle anymore,” John said. “Everything is quiet.”

The elder surprised him by telling him to return to prayer and ask that the struggle be restored. In the difficulty, he explained, John had been learning humility, dependence, and endurance. Without realizing it, the very thing he wanted removed had also been shaping him.

So John prayed again, and when the struggle returned, his prayer changed. He no longer asked God to make the conflict disappear. Instead, he asked for strength to remain faithful within it.

### Devotional

Most of us instinctively imagine peace as the absence of struggle. If anxiety disappeared, if desire quieted, if anger stopped rising, or if temptation finally lost its voice, then perhaps we could become the people we are trying to be. We naturally pray for troublesome things to go away.

John prayed that way too, and for a while his prayer seemed to work. What makes this saying unsettling is the elder’s response, because he does not celebrate John’s freedom from conflict as an unquestioned victory. He recognizes that something important has disappeared along with the struggle.

The difficulty had been teaching John that he needed grace. It had been revealing the limits of his own strength, drawing him into dependence, and keeping him close to the ground. The battle itself was not good, but something good had been growing in the way John was learning to meet it.

This does not mean that suffering is inherently valuable or that we should seek out unnecessary hardship. There are wounds that need healing, dangers that should be escaped, and burdens that should be removed when they can be. The desert elders were not asking people to romanticize pain.

But they understood that some forms of struggle cannot simply be removed from human life. In those places, the question may slowly change from *How do I make this stop?* to *How do I live faithfully while this is here?* That is a very different prayer.

We often imagine transformation as no longer experiencing the things that once troubled us. Sometimes that may happen, but there is another kind of transformation in which the old impulse still appears and yet no longer commands us. The desire comes, but there is more space around it; anger rises, but we do not immediately become its servant; fear speaks, but another voice has become possible.

The struggle may remain while our relationship to it changes. What once felt like proof of failure can become a place of practice, where we learn patience, honesty, restraint, and dependence. We may still feel the pull, but the pull no longer has the same authority.

John eventually stops asking for a life without resistance. His prayer becomes simpler and more honest: give me strength for what is here. There is humility in such a prayer because it does not demand control over the conditions of our transformation.

It allows even difficulty to become a teacher. The struggle shows us where we still cling, where we still fear, where we still need help, and where compassion for other struggling people might grow. What humiliates us can sometimes soften us, making us less certain of our own strength and more patient with the weakness of others.

Perhaps peace is not always the disappearance of the battle. Sometimes peace is discovering that the battle no longer has to frighten us, because we have learned that we can meet what comes without immediately surrendering to it. We can ask for help, fall and begin again, and slowly become freer even while the struggle remains.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Give me strength for this moment.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me through the struggle.*
