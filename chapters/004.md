## Day 4 — Strength for the Struggle

### Abba John the Dwarf

Abba Poemen told a story about John the Dwarf.

John had grown weary of the passions that troubled him, so he prayed that God would take them away. In time, the inner conflict became quiet. The thoughts and desires that had pressed against him seemed to lose their strength, and John found himself living in an unfamiliar peace.

He went to an elder and told him what had happened.

“I have no battle anymore,” John said. “Everything is quiet.”

But the elder surprised him. He told John to return to prayer and ask that the struggle be restored. In the difficulty, the elder explained, John had been learning humility, dependence, and endurance. Without realizing it, the very thing he wanted removed had been shaping him.

So John prayed again.

When the struggle returned, he did not ask God to make it disappear.

Instead he prayed, “Lord, give me strength to remain faithful in the fight.”

### Devotional

Most of us instinctively imagine peace as the absence of struggle. If anxiety disappeared, if desire quieted, if anger stopped rising, if temptation finally lost its voice, then perhaps we could become the people we are trying to be. We naturally pray for troublesome things to go away.

John prayed that way too.

And for a while, his prayer seemed to work.

What makes this saying unsettling is the elder's response. He does not celebrate John's freedom from conflict. Instead, he recognizes that something important has disappeared along with it. The struggle had been teaching John that he needed grace. It had been revealing the limits of his own strength and keeping him close to the ground.

This does not mean that suffering is inherently good, or that we should seek out unnecessary hardship. The desert elders were not romanticizing every pain. But they understood that there are forms of struggle we cannot simply remove from human life. In those places, the question slowly changes from *How do I make this stop?* to *How do I live faithfully while this is here?*

That is a very different prayer.

We often believe transformation means no longer experiencing the things that once troubled us. Perhaps sometimes it does. But there is another kind of transformation in which the old impulse still appears and yet no longer commands us. The desire comes, but there is space around it. Anger rises, but we do not immediately become its servant. Fear speaks, but another voice has become possible.

The struggle may remain while our relationship to it changes.

John eventually stops asking for a life without resistance. His prayer becomes simpler and more honest: give me strength for what is here.

There is humility in such a prayer because it does not demand control over the conditions of our transformation. It allows even difficulty to become a teacher. The struggle shows us where we still cling, where we still fear, where we still need help, and where compassion for other struggling people might grow.

Perhaps peace is not always the disappearance of the battle.

Sometimes peace is discovering that the battle no longer has to frighten us.

We can meet what comes. We can ask for help. We can fall and begin again. And slowly, even here, something in us is being made free.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Give me strength for this moment.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me through the struggle.*
