## Day 1 — A Rhythm That Carries Us

### Abba Anthony the Great

When Abba Anthony was living in the desert, he became troubled by distracting and sinful thoughts. He cried out to God, asking how he could be saved when his mind would not leave him in peace.

Going outside, he saw someone like himself sitting at work, then rising to pray, then sitting again to weave a rope, and then rising once more to pray. It was an angel sent to encourage him.

“Do this,” the angel told him, “and you will be saved.”

Anthony was filled with courage. He followed the pattern he had been shown.

### Devotional

Anthony goes into the desert seeking God and discovers that he has brought his mind with him. The silence does not immediately become peaceful. Instead, without the ordinary distractions of life, thoughts seem to grow louder, until Anthony finally cries out in frustration: *I want to be saved, but these thoughts will not leave me alone.*

There is something wonderfully reassuring about the answer he receives. God does not give Anthony a technique for eliminating thoughts. The angel does not explain why they are there or teach him how to reach some permanent state of interior tranquility. He simply shows Anthony how to live while the thoughts are present.

Work for a while. Pray for a while. Return to the work. Then return again to prayer.

Perhaps Anthony had imagined that salvation would feel like finally conquering the chaos within him. Instead, he is given a rhythm. The path forward is not to wait until his mind becomes perfectly quiet, but to keep returning—to the rope in his hands, to the prayer of his heart, to the simple thing that is before him.

We often make the same mistake Anthony seems to have made. We assume that spiritual maturity means eventually reaching a condition in which distraction, anxiety, temptation, boredom, anger, or craving no longer trouble us. When they appear again, we interpret their presence as evidence that something has gone wrong.

But the angel does not seem particularly alarmed by Anthony's thoughts. His answer suggests that the important thing is not whether thoughts arise but what Anthony does when they arise. He can become entangled in them, endlessly examining and fighting them, or he can return to the life immediately in front of him.

There is wisdom here for those of us who live far from the Egyptian desert. Our rope may be an email, a sink full of dishes, a child who needs attention, a piece of code, a walk around the neighborhood, or a meal being prepared. These ordinary things can become anchors to the present moment, places where we stop trying to solve ourselves and simply participate in the life we have been given.

And then, periodically, we rise.

We remember God. We breathe. We pray. We become aware again of the Presence in which all our thoughts are occurring. Then we return to our ordinary work.

Perhaps this is one of the first lessons of the desert: we do not have to become perfectly still before we can begin walking toward God. Sometimes salvation looks much less dramatic. We work, we pray, we wander, and we return.

Again and again.
