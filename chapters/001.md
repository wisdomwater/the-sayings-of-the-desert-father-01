## Day 1 — A Rhythm That Carries Us

### Abba Anthony the Great

When Abba Anthony was living in the desert, he became troubled by distracting and sinful thoughts. He cried out to God, asking how he could be saved when his mind would not leave him in peace. The silence he had sought did not immediately bring the stillness he expected.

Going outside, Anthony saw someone like himself sitting at work, then rising to pray. Afterward the man sat down again to weave a rope, then rose once more to pray. Anthony understood that an angel had been sent to show him a way through his distress.

“Do this,” the angel told him, “and you will be saved.” Anthony was filled with courage and began following the rhythm he had been shown. He worked, he prayed, and then he returned to the work before him.

### Devotional

Anthony goes into the desert seeking God and discovers that he has brought his mind with him. The silence does not immediately become peaceful, and without the ordinary distractions of life his thoughts seem to grow louder. Eventually he cries out in frustration, wanting to know how he can be saved when his own mind will not leave him alone.

There is something wonderfully reassuring about the answer he receives. God does not give Anthony a technique for eliminating his thoughts, nor does the angel explain why they are there or teach him how to reach some permanent state of interior tranquility. Instead, Anthony is shown how to live faithfully while the thoughts are still present.

He works for a while and then he prays. He returns to his work and, after a time, returns again to prayer. What he receives is not an escape from his restless mind, but a rhythm capable of carrying him through it.

Perhaps Anthony had imagined that salvation would feel like finally conquering the chaos within him. Instead, he discovers that the path forward does not require waiting until his mind becomes perfectly quiet. He can return to the rope in his hands, to the prayer of his heart, and to the simple reality immediately before him.

We often make the same mistake. We imagine that spiritual maturity should eventually produce a condition in which distraction, anxiety, temptation, boredom, anger, or craving no longer troubles us. When one of these old movements appears again, we can interpret its return as evidence that our practice has failed.

But the angel does not seem particularly alarmed by Anthony's thoughts. The response suggests that the important question is not whether thoughts arise, but what Anthony does once they are present. He can become entangled in them, endlessly examining, resisting, and negotiating with them, or he can gently return to the life that is actually happening.

There is wisdom here for those of us who live far from the Egyptian desert. Our rope may be an email, a sink full of dishes, a child who needs attention, a piece of code, a walk around the neighborhood, or a meal being prepared. Ordinary work can become an anchor to the present, a place where we stop trying to solve ourselves and simply participate in the life we have been given.

Then, periodically, we rise again into remembrance. We pause, breathe, pray, and become aware once more of the Presence within which all our thoughts and activities are occurring. Afterward, we return to the ordinary task, carrying that awareness with us as best we can.

Perhaps this is one of the first lessons of the desert. We do not have to become perfectly still before we can begin walking toward God, and we do not need to eliminate every wandering thought before prayer becomes real. Sometimes salvation looks much less dramatic: we work, we pray, we wander, and we return, allowing that simple rhythm to carry us again and again.
