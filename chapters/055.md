## Day 55 — A Place for Mourning

### Abba Poemen

A brother came to Abba Poemen and asked what he should do. Poemen reminded him that when Abraham entered the promised land, he first purchased a tomb for himself. Through that burial place, Poemen said, Abraham came to possess the land.

The brother asked what this tomb meant for the spiritual life. Poemen answered that it was a place of weeping and mourning. Before trying to possess anything greater, the monk needed somewhere within himself where grief could be honestly received.

### Devotional

We usually imagine spiritual growth as an accumulation. We want more peace, more wisdom, more faith, more clarity, and perhaps a deeper sense of God’s presence. Poemen begins somewhere unexpected: with a place to grieve.

His image comes from Abraham entering the land that had been promised to him. Before that promise was fully realized, Abraham acquired a burial place for Sarah. Poemen sees something spiritually significant in the fact that the first piece of ground Abraham possessed was connected with mourning.

There are parts of life that cannot be healed by immediately turning them into lessons. Loss, disappointment, regret, failure, and suffering often need to be felt before they can be interpreted. When we hurry toward meaning, we may accidentally build over grief that is still asking to be acknowledged.

A place of mourning allows us to stop doing that. It gives sorrow permission to exist without demanding that it quickly become gratitude, wisdom, or hope. We can tell the truth that something hurt, something was lost, or something did not become what we hoped. Such honesty is not the opposite of faith.

The Desert Fathers often spoke of tears as part of repentance, but repentance was larger than self-condemnation. Tears could arise when someone finally saw clearly what had been happening within them. Sorrow softened what defensiveness had kept hard.

There is a difference between mourning and shame. Shame tells us that our failure defines us and should drive us into hiding. Mourning allows us to feel the pain of what has happened while remaining present to God. One closes the heart, while the other can open it.

We also carry grief that has nothing to do with our own wrongdoing. Some losses simply happen to us, and some wounds are caused by circumstances or by other people. A spiritual life that has no room for lament eventually asks us to deny part of our experience.

Poemen gives grief a place in the landscape. It does not have to occupy everything, but neither must it be pushed outside the boundaries of spiritual life. There can be a room within us where sorrow is allowed to sit before God. We do not have to evict it in order to appear faithful.

This may also explain why grief can sometimes deepen compassion. When we have allowed ourselves to mourn, another person’s pain becomes less threatening. We no longer need to silence their sorrow because it reminds us of our own. Our wounds can become places through which we recognize one another.

The image of a tomb also reminds us that some things must be released. We may continue carrying an old identity, expectation, relationship, resentment, or imagined future long after it has ceased to be alive. Mourning acknowledges that something has ended. Only then can we begin to discover what life looks like without it.

That does not mean grief follows a tidy sequence. We may return to the same sorrow many times and find that it speaks differently as we change. The place of mourning remains available when we need it. Returning there is not necessarily regression.

Poemen suggests that something can be received through this very ground. The place where we finally stop resisting sorrow may become part of the land in which a deeper life takes root. We do not acquire that life by escaping grief. Sometimes we enter it by allowing ourselves to mourn.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Give my sorrow a place before you.*

**Breathing out:** *Meet me there with your presence.*
