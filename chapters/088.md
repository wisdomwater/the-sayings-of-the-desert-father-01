## Day 88 — Keeping Appetite in Its Place

### Abba Pior

Abba Pior had an unusual habit of walking while he ate. When someone asked why, he explained that he did not want eating to become an occupation, but something secondary to the rest of his life. Asked again, he said he did not want the pleasure of eating to gain too strong a hold over him. His practice was severe, but its purpose was to keep appetite from becoming the organizing center of his day.

Pior was not claiming that the body could live without food. He ate because nourishment was necessary, while deliberately resisting the temptation to make nourishment his primary source of satisfaction. The discipline was less about rejecting food than about changing his relationship to it. He wanted to receive what was needed without becoming preoccupied with receiving more.

### Devotional

Appetite has a way of expanding beyond its original purpose. Hunger asks for nourishment, but desire can quickly begin asking for stimulation, comfort, novelty, and repetition. What began as something necessary can gradually occupy much more attention than necessity requires.

Pior responds with an asceticism that may feel foreign to us. We do not need to imitate his refusal of bodily pleasure or assume that enjoying a meal is somehow spiritually suspect. Yet the question beneath his practice remains surprisingly contemporary.

How much space does appetite occupy in our inner life? We can spend significant energy anticipating what we will eat, buy, watch, scroll through, or experience next. Pleasure itself is not the problem, but it can quietly become the axis around which attention turns.

There is freedom in being able to enjoy something without needing it to become more. A good meal can simply be a good meal, received with gratitude and then released. Satisfaction does not have to immediately generate another search for satisfaction.

This is especially important because appetites rarely announce when they have crossed that boundary. The shift from enjoyment to preoccupation is usually gradual. Attention begins returning again and again to the next opportunity for relief or pleasure.

Pior’s strange little practice therefore asks a useful question about proportion. Is this thing serving life, or is life increasingly being arranged around this thing? That question can be asked about food, entertainment, possessions, achievement, attention, or anything else capable of becoming more central than we intended.

Christian freedom need not mean becoming suspicious of the body. It can mean allowing bodily pleasures to remain gifts rather than masters. We can enjoy what is good while refusing to ask it to carry the weight of our deepest hunger.

Pior invites us to notice what has become too important. We can receive what we need, delight in what is given, and then turn our attention again toward the larger life surrounding it. Appetite can have a place without becoming the center.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Teach me to receive with gratitude.*

**Breathing out:** *Keep my desires in their proper place.*
