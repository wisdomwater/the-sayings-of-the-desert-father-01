## Day 35 — Protect the Relationship

### Abba Achilles

Three old men once came to Abba Achilles, and one of them carried a bad reputation. The first two asked Achilles to make them fishing nets, but he declined. When the third man asked, Achilles immediately agreed.

Later, the other two questioned him about the difference. Achilles explained that they would not have been hurt by his refusal, because they trusted that he simply lacked the time. But the third man might have assumed that Achilles had heard about his sin and rejected him because of it.

So Achilles chose differently.

He made the net in order to protect the relationship. He wanted the brother’s heart strengthened rather than pushed further into grief. Mercy, in this case, looked like taking special care around an already wounded place.

### Devotional

Reputation can become a second punishment.

Something happens, a person fails, and the story begins traveling ahead of them. Before they enter the room, people already know what to think.

That changes every interaction.

A neutral expression can feel like disapproval. A delayed response can feel like rejection, and an ordinary boundary can feel like confirmation that everyone now sees only the failure. Once shame has entered a person’s life, even small gestures carry more weight.

Achilles understands this.

He does not pretend the reputation is imaginary. He knows exactly why the brother might interpret rejection differently from the others. Instead of insisting that everyone be treated identically, he responds according to what each person can actually bear.

That is a subtle form of mercy.

Equality is not always sameness. Sometimes love means recognizing that one person is standing on bruised ground and stepping more carefully there. A gesture that would mean nothing to someone else may carry enormous meaning for the person already expecting rejection.

We often underestimate this.

Once someone has failed publicly, we may assume they need correction most of all. But they may already be correcting themselves relentlessly. What they lack may not be awareness of their failure, but evidence that relationship is still possible.

Achilles gives that evidence.

He does not deliver a lecture about forgiveness. He makes a fishing net. The act itself says, *I have not withdrawn from you.*

That message can be healing.

Shame tells a person that the worst thing they have done has become the truest thing about them. It whispers that every kindness is temporary and every relationship will eventually disappear once people know enough. Mercy interrupts that story through ordinary faithfulness.

This does not require ignoring patterns of harm.

There are situations where trust must be rebuilt slowly and where boundaries are necessary. Protecting relationship does not mean pretending that nothing happened. It means refusing to use another person’s weakness as a reason to withhold basic human regard.

Achilles also shows remarkable attentiveness.

He imagines how his response might land in the other person’s heart. That is different from being controlled by another person’s feelings. It is simply the willingness to remember that our actions do not occur in a vacuum.

Mercy asks us to notice context.

The same word can strengthen one person and crush another. The same silence can feel peaceful to one and abandoning to someone else. Wisdom learns to see the person rather than merely applying the same response every time.

Perhaps this is part of what it means to carry one another.

We become careful around places where someone has already been wounded. We refuse to deepen shame unnecessarily, and we look for small ways to keep the bridge intact. Sometimes the most spiritual thing we can do is make the net.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Make me attentive to another’s wound.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to protect the bond.*
