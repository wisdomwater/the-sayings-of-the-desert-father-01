## Day 61 — The Labor of Prayer

### Abba Agathon

The brothers asked Abba Agathon which good work required the greatest effort. He answered that nothing demanded more labor than prayer to God. Whenever a person tries to pray, resistance rises to distract, discourage, or pull the mind away.

Agathon observed that many kinds of work eventually become easier through practice. Prayer is different because it keeps asking for renewed attention, humility, and return. For him, prayer remained a lifelong struggle, not something a person simply mastered and moved beyond.

### Devotional

Prayer can sound effortless when described from a distance. We imagine stillness, peace, clarity, and a mind naturally turned toward God. Anyone who actually prays for long discovers how quickly the mind wanders.

Agathon does not seem surprised by this difficulty. He expects resistance and treats it as part of the work itself. The struggle to return is not evidence that prayer has failed.

This can change the way we judge our own practice. We may sit down to pray and immediately remember something we need to do, replay a conversation, or begin planning the day. Each distraction can feel like proof that we are doing it badly.

Agathon offers another interpretation. The repeated return may actually be the substance of prayer. Attention wanders, we notice, and we turn again toward God.

This is why prayer can feel strangely tiring. It asks us to release the constant momentum of thought without demanding that thought disappear. We keep choosing presence even while the mind offers countless alternatives.

The labor is not about forcing ourselves into a perfect spiritual state. It is the quieter work of remaining available. We sit, listen, breathe, speak honestly, and begin again when we discover we have drifted.

Over time, this kind of prayer can reshape ordinary life. The same movement of noticing and returning becomes possible in conversation, work, conflict, and desire. Prayer teaches the heart how to come back.

Agathon also reminds us that progress does not mean reaching a point where effort disappears. Some practices become easier because the skill is learned and the body adapts. Prayer remains relational, and relationship cannot be reduced to a completed technique.

There will be days when prayer feels alive and days when it feels empty. There will be moments of clarity and long stretches of distraction. Faithfulness may simply mean continuing to show up without demanding a particular experience.

This keeps prayer from becoming another performance. We do not need to prove that we are calm, focused, or spiritually advanced. We only need to bring the mind and heart we actually have.

Agathon’s teaching is demanding, but it is also reassuring. If prayer remains difficult, that does not mean we are failing at it. The work is to keep returning, one breath and one moment at a time.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Draw my attention back to you.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to return again.*
