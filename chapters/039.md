## Day 39 — Help Them Rise

### Abba Poemen

A brother in a monastery fell into sin, and the community consulted an old anchorite about what should be done. The elder told them to send the brother away, so he left the monastery and sat alone in a ravine, overwhelmed with grief. When some monks later found him there and told Abba Poemen what had happened, Poemen immediately asked them to bring the brother to him.

When the brother arrived, Poemen rose to meet him, embraced him, and treated him with kindness. He invited him to eat rather than questioning him about his failure. Only afterward did Poemen turn his attention to the elder who had ordered the brother away.

Poemen invited that elder to visit and offered him a simple image. Two people each had a dead person in their own house, but one left his own dead unattended and went to mourn over the dead in his neighbor’s house. The elder understood the meaning and recognized that he had become more occupied with another person’s sin than with his own need for repentance.

### Devotional

There are many ways to respond when someone falls.

We can investigate what happened, determine responsibility, establish consequences, or explain what should have been done differently. Some of those things may eventually be necessary. But Poemen begins somewhere else.

He begins with the person on the ground.

The brother already knows that he has failed. He has been expelled from his community and sits alone, weeping in a ravine. At that moment, another verdict would add very little.

So Poemen embraces him.

That gesture does not declare the brother innocent. It does not erase whatever happened or suggest that actions carry no consequences. It simply communicates that failure has not made him untouchable.

This distinction is crucial.

When someone falls, we can become so concerned with making sure they understand the seriousness of what they have done that we forget to ask whether they can still imagine getting up. Shame has a way of turning a failure into an identity. It says not merely, *You did something wrong,* but, *This is what you are now.*

Poemen refuses to cooperate with that message.

His first response says, *You still belong among human beings.* There is food for you, an embrace for you, and a place where you can sit. The path forward begins by restoring enough dignity for the person to walk it.

Mercy is not opposed to accountability.

In fact, mercy may make genuine accountability possible. A person who believes they are permanently condemned has little reason to change, but a person who believes restoration remains possible can begin facing the truth without being destroyed by it.

Poemen then turns toward the condemning elder.

His correction is gentle but penetrating. Why are you weeping over another person’s dead while your own lies unattended? Why has someone else’s failure become more interesting to you than your own unfinished heart?

The image exposes something familiar.

Another person’s obvious failure can make us feel temporarily secure. Their mistake is visible, while ours may be quieter, more respectable, or easier to explain. We begin helping them repent while forgetting that we too remain in need of transformation.

Poemen redirects the work.

Before asking how another person could have done such a thing, remember what remains unresolved in yourself. Before deciding whether someone deserves another chance, remember how many chances have shaped your own life. Self-knowledge creates space for mercy.

There will still be situations where boundaries matter.

Sometimes a person cannot immediately return to the role, relationship, or responsibility they once held. Trust may need time, and consequences may remain. But consequences and abandonment are not the same thing.

We can hold a boundary and still reach toward someone.

We can say that something was wrong while refusing to say that the person is beyond hope. We can protect what needs protecting without turning punishment into exile from the possibility of grace.

Perhaps this is the heart of Poemen’s response.

When someone has fallen, the first question need not be, *How do we make sure they understand how far they fell?* They may already know. The more merciful question may be, *What will help them stand again?*

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Teach me to see the fallen with mercy.*

**Breathing out:** *Help me lift rather than condemn.*
