## Day 85 — What You Choose Not to Collect

### Abba Macarius the Great

It was said of Abba Macarius that he had become like God upon the earth in the way he dealt with other people’s faults. When he saw someone fail, he acted as though he had not seen it. When he heard something shameful about another person, he acted as though he had not heard it. He refused to gather another person’s weakness into a collection he could later use against them.

This did not mean that Macarius believed wrongdoing was unreal or unimportant. The saying emphasizes instead what he chose to carry in his own mind and heart. He did not make another person’s failures into possessions. Mercy shaped what he allowed himself to retain.

### Devotional

We collect more about other people than we realize. A careless remark, an embarrassing mistake, a moment of selfishness, or an old failure can remain stored in memory long after the event has passed. Over time, these fragments can quietly become our definition of the person.

Macarius practices another kind of attention. He does not deny what happened, but he refuses to keep staring at it. He will not make another person’s worst moment the lens through which everything else is interpreted.

There is generosity in allowing some things to pass through us without taking root. Not every fault needs to be repeated, analyzed, remembered, or shared. Sometimes mercy consists simply in declining to preserve what does not need preserving.

This becomes especially difficult when the fault confirms something we already believe about someone. The mind loves evidence that strengthens its judgments. Once we begin collecting offenses, almost anything can be added to the pile.

Covering a fault is not the same as concealing abuse or ignoring harm that requires intervention. Some actions must be confronted, boundaries may be necessary, and truth sometimes needs to be spoken clearly. Macarius is addressing the ordinary human tendency to notice weakness eagerly and mercy reluctantly.

His way of seeing asks us to remember that people are always larger than the fragments we observe. A person can fail badly and still possess goodness, possibility, and dignity. Mercy leaves room for a story that has not finished yet.

We might notice how often we rehearse another person’s shortcomings internally. Each repetition can deepen the grooves of resentment and make compassion more difficult. Choosing not to rehearse the offense can become a quiet form of spiritual freedom.

Macarius invites us to become careful curators of what we carry. We cannot always control what we see or hear, but we can decide what we keep feeding with attention. Some things are better allowed to disappear rather than become part of the permanent archive of the heart.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Teach me to see with mercy.*

**Breathing out:** *Help me release what I need not keep.*
