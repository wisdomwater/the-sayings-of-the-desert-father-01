## Day 21 — The Way Through

### Abba Anthony

Abba Anthony once saw, in a vision, snares spread across the whole earth. They seemed to be everywhere, waiting to catch anyone who tried to move through them. Overwhelmed by what he saw, Anthony cried out, asking who could possibly escape so many traps.

A voice answered him with a single word: humility.

Anthony was not told that wisdom would avoid every snare, or that strength would overpower them all. The way through was something quieter. Humility could pass where self-confidence might become entangled.

### Devotional

Humility is easy to misunderstand. We may hear the word and imagine thinking poorly of ourselves, minimizing our gifts, or becoming passive in the face of other people. But that kind of self-erasure is not what Anthony is pointing toward.

The snares in his vision seem to represent the countless ways the heart can become trapped. Pride, fear, anger, desire, comparison, self-justification, and the need to be admired can all catch us precisely because something in us insists on maintaining a particular image of ourselves. The trap tightens when we believe we must always be right, strong, impressive, secure, or in control.

Humility loosens that grip.

A humble person can say, *I may be wrong*, without feeling that the entire self is collapsing. They can receive correction without immediately building a defense, admit weakness without turning weakness into shame, and allow another person to know something they do not. Humility creates room for reality to be larger than the version of it we would prefer.

This is why humility can move through places where strength alone becomes entangled. Strength often tries to conquer the obstacle, while humility asks whether conquest is even necessary. Sometimes the snare is not the difficulty itself, but our insistence that we should never have to experience it.

We may become trapped by the need to prove that we are good people. Then a criticism arrives, and instead of listening for whatever truth it might contain, we begin defending our character. The original issue may have been small, but the need to protect our identity makes it enormous.

Humility offers another possibility. We can let the criticism touch us without allowing it to define us, keeping what is true and releasing what is not. We do not have to become smaller in order to stop defending an inflated version of ourselves.

The same is true of failure. When we imagine that maturity means being beyond certain struggles, their return becomes humiliating in the worst sense of the word. But genuine humility simply says, *This is where I am today*, and begins from there.

There is great freedom in that honesty. We no longer have to hide the weakness, exaggerate the strength, or compare our path with someone else’s. We can meet the present moment without spending so much energy protecting the story of who we think we ought to be.

Perhaps this is how humility slips through the snares. It gives them less to catch.

The person who does not need to appear invulnerable can admit fear. The person who does not need to appear wise can ask a question, and the person who does not need to win can sometimes let an argument pass without following it to the end.

Anthony’s vision does not promise a world without traps. It offers a way of walking through one. We travel more lightly when there is less self-image to defend and less certainty to clutch.

Humility is not becoming nothing. It is becoming free enough to stand in what is true without needing it to make us greater or smaller than we are.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Teach me what is true.*

**Breathing out:** *Free me from the need to defend myself.*
