## Day 9 — Get Up Again

### Abba Sisoes

A brother once came to Abba Sisoes carrying the weight of failure. He told the old man that he had fallen and asked what he should do. Sisoes answered simply: get up again.

The brother did as he was told, but after some time he returned discouraged. He had risen, tried again, and fallen once more. Sisoes gave him the same answer: rise again.

At last the brother asked how long this could continue. How many times could a person fall and still keep beginning? Sisoes did not give him a number, only a direction: keep rising until the end.

### Devotional

There is something almost startling about the simplicity of Sisoes’s answer. The brother seems to want a different solution the second time because he has already tried getting up. It did not solve the problem permanently, and now he is back where he started.

Surely, he seems to think, there must be another answer.

Sisoes gives him the same one.

Get up again.

This challenges the way many of us imagine transformation. We often picture growth as a steady ascent in which insight leads to progress, progress leads to stability, and stability eventually carries us beyond the old struggle. When we fall back into something familiar, we can interpret the relapse as proof that the earlier growth was never real.

Sisoes measures the situation differently. He does not define the brother by the fact that he fell. He pays attention to whether the brother is still willing to rise.

That distinction may change the way we understand spiritual maturity. Perhaps maturity is not becoming someone who is never knocked down again. Perhaps it is becoming someone who refuses to make the ground a permanent home.

We fall into anger and notice it sooner. We fall into fear and remember more quickly where to turn. We find ourselves again inside resentment, distraction, pride, desire, or despair, and something within us begins to recognize the landscape.

Then we rise.

There may be a hidden humility in repeated rising. The first time we make a change, we may imagine that we have finally conquered ourselves. After the fifth or fiftieth return, we begin to understand that transformation depends less on heroic willpower than on patience, grace, and the willingness to begin again.

Repeated failure can tempt us toward despair, but despair can carry its own form of pride. It assumes that by now we ought to be beyond this, as though our disappointment proves that we expected to become permanently invulnerable. Sisoes offers no such expectation.

He simply tells us to get up.

This does not make our choices meaningless or turn repentance into something casual. Rising may include apology, repair, changing our habits, asking for help, or removing ourselves from conditions that repeatedly lead us into the same harm. But shame adds nothing necessary to that work.

Shame tells us to stay on the ground because falling has finally revealed who we really are. Sisoes refuses that conclusion. Who we are is revealed not only by the moment we fall, but also by what we do next.

Perhaps the spiritual life is less like achieving permanent balance and more like developing a deep memory of the way home. Even after wandering, even after failing, and even after discovering that an old weakness is not as dead as we hoped, the path remains open.

Stand up.

Turn toward God.

Take the next step.

And if tomorrow finds you on the ground again, the invitation will still be there. The mercy of beginning has not been exhausted.

Get up again.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *When I fall, help me rise.*

**Breathing out:** *I return to you again.*
