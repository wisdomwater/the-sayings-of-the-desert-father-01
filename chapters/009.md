## Day 9 — Get Up Again

### Abba Sisoes

A brother came to Abba Sisoes burdened by failure.

“Abba,” he said, “I have fallen. What should I do?”

Sisoes answered simply.

“Get up again.”

The brother returned to him later.

“I got up,” he said, “but I have fallen again.”

“Then get up again.”

The brother was discouraged.

“How many times must I keep doing this?”

Sisoes did not give him a number.

“Again and again,” he told him. “Keep rising until the end.”

### Devotional

There is something almost shocking about the simplicity of Sisoes's answer.

The brother seems to want a different solution the second time. He has already tried getting up. It did not work, at least not permanently. He rose, walked for a while, and found himself on the ground again.

Surely now a more sophisticated answer is required.

Sisoes gives him the same one.

Get up.

This can be difficult for those of us who imagine transformation as a steadily ascending path. We expect that once we understand something, we should no longer struggle with it. Once we repent, we should never return to the same pattern. Once we have experienced freedom, falling back into an old habit can feel like proof that the freedom was never real.

But Sisoes does not measure the brother by whether he falls.

He pays attention to whether he rises.

That distinction may change the way we understand spiritual maturity. Perhaps maturity is not a state in which we are never again knocked down. Perhaps it is the growing refusal to make the ground our home.

We fall into anger and notice it sooner.

We fall into fear and remember where to turn.

We become consumed by desire, distraction, resentment, pride, or despair, and eventually something within us whispers, *You do not have to remain here.*

So we rise.

There may be humility hidden in repeated rising. The first time we change, we may imagine that we have finally conquered ourselves. The fifth or fiftieth time, we begin to understand that transformation depends less on heroic willpower than on grace, patience, and the willingness to return.

Repeated failure can tempt us toward despair, but despair carries its own strange form of pride. It assumes that by now we ought to be beyond this. Sisoes offers no such expectation.

He simply says: get up again.

This does not make our choices meaningless. Rising includes repentance, learning, repair, and sometimes changing the conditions that keep leading us toward the same fall. But shame adds nothing useful to that work. Shame tells us to remain on the ground because falling has revealed who we really are.

Sisoes refuses that conclusion.

Who we are is not revealed only by the moment we fall.

It is also revealed by what we do next.

Perhaps the spiritual life is less like achieving permanent balance and more like developing a deep memory of the way home. Even after wandering, even after failing, even after discovering that an old weakness is not as dead as we hoped, the path remains.

Stand up.

Turn toward God.

Take the next step.

And if tomorrow finds you on the ground again, the invitation will still be waiting.

Get up again.
