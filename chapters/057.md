# Day 57 — What We Become Accustomed To

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen named three things that can bring a person down: self-will, bodily ease, and becoming accustomed to them. His concern was not with a single dramatic failure but with habits that quietly become normal. What begins as a preference can slowly become something we no longer question.

Poemen places particular weight on becoming accustomed to these things. Repetition gives them a kind of authority over us. Before long, what once felt like a choice begins to feel simply like the way we live.

### Devotional

We usually notice the dramatic decisions in our lives more easily than the repeated small ones. A major failure gets our attention, while hundreds of quiet choices can pass almost unnoticed. Poemen directs our attention toward what repetition is slowly teaching us to become.

Habits are powerful because they reduce the need for conscious choice. What we do repeatedly begins to happen almost automatically. This can be a gift when the habit is good, but it can also allow patterns to deepen without much resistance.

Poemen first names self-will. We encountered this already as the insistence that things must happen according to our preference. When that insistence becomes habitual, we may stop recognizing it as self-will at all. We simply assume that our way is the reasonable way and experience resistance as an inconvenience or threat.

He also names bodily ease. Rest itself is not the enemy, and the Desert Fathers could sometimes be surprisingly flexible about human limits. The danger lies in becoming governed by comfort, arranging life so that discomfort is always treated as something that must immediately be removed. When ease becomes an expectation, even ordinary difficulty can feel intolerable.

The third element may be the most important: becoming accustomed. A pattern repeated often enough begins to shape what feels normal. We can become accustomed to distraction, irritation, overeating, avoidance, resentment, hurry, or constant stimulation. Eventually we may stop experiencing these things as choices because they have become part of the background of life.

This works in the other direction too. We can become accustomed to prayer, patience, honesty, movement, attention, generosity, or pausing before reacting. At first these may require deliberate effort, but repetition gradually changes what comes naturally. Formation happens through what we practice.

That is why change often begins smaller than we expect. We may imagine that freedom requires a dramatic act of will, but sometimes it begins with interrupting one familiar sequence. We notice the cue, pause before the automatic response, and make one different choice. That interruption reminds us that the pattern is not inevitable.

There is compassion in seeing habit this way. We do not need to treat every repeated struggle as proof of moral failure. Some behaviors have simply been rehearsed so many times that they have become deeply familiar. Recognizing the pattern allows us to work with it more patiently and deliberately.

Poemen’s warning is therefore not only about avoiding bad behavior. It is about paying attention to what repeated behavior is forming within us. Each habit is quietly teaching the body and mind what to expect, what to seek, and how to respond. Over time, those expectations become part of our character.

The spiritual question becomes very practical. What am I becoming accustomed to? Which patterns are making me more free, present, and loving, and which are narrowing my life? The answer may reveal more about our direction than our intentions alone.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me what is shaping me.*

**Breathing out:** *Form in me what leads to life.*
