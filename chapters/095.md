## Day 95 — The Gift of Today

### Abba Macarius the Great

As Abba Macarius grew older, the elders living in the mountains sent word asking him to visit them. They knew that his life was drawing toward its end, and they wanted an opportunity to hear his wisdom while he was still among them. Rather than requiring everyone to make the difficult journey to his cell, Macarius agreed to come. When he arrived, the brothers gathered around him and asked him to offer a final word of spiritual counsel.

Macarius did not give them an elaborate teaching or recount the accomplishments of his long life. Instead, he urged them to weep while there was still time, warning of a place where tears would bring only suffering. His words reflected the urgency with which the desert monks regarded repentance and the seriousness of the choices made during earthly life. The brothers were deeply moved, falling to the ground in tears and asking Macarius to pray for them.

### Devotional

There is a peculiar way we relate to time when we believe we have plenty of it. We postpone difficult conversations, delay changes we know we need to make, and assume that tomorrow will provide a more convenient opportunity to begin. Even our spiritual aspirations can become promises directed toward some imagined future version of ourselves. We intend to become more patient, more generous, or more attentive to God, but not necessarily today.

Macarius speaks with an urgency that can feel uncomfortable to modern ears. His warning reflects a religious world in which death, judgment, and the possibility of eternal punishment were taken with extraordinary seriousness. He does not soften these concerns or present repentance as something that can be indefinitely postponed. For him, the present moment carries a significance that cannot simply be transferred to tomorrow.

We may struggle with the severity of his imagery, especially if we have encountered religious teachings that rely upon fear to produce obedience. The idea of tears becoming an instrument of punishment can seem difficult to reconcile with a God whose deepest nature is love. Yet we need not adopt every assumption about divine judgment to recognize the importance of Macarius's invitation. Beneath the frightening imagery lies a simple question: Why postpone the healing that is available to us now?

Repentance is often misunderstood as a prolonged exercise in regret. We imagine revisiting our failures, rehearsing our guilt, and somehow proving the sincerity of our sorrow through emotional suffering. But genuine repentance is directed toward transformation rather than punishment. Its purpose is to help us recognize where we have wandered from love and begin moving toward it again.

Consider how easily we postpone reconciliation with someone we have hurt. We may tell ourselves that we need more time to find the right words or wait until the circumstances feel less uncomfortable. Sometimes patience is genuinely necessary, but at other times our delay simply protects us from vulnerability. The longer we wait, the more difficult the distance can become to cross.

The same pattern appears in our relationship with ourselves. We notice habits that leave us restless, resentments that consume our attention, or ways of living that seem increasingly disconnected from our deepest values. Yet we convince ourselves that meaningful change must wait until life becomes less complicated. We imagine that some future season will finally provide the clarity and strength we currently lack.

Macarius reminds us that the present moment is already a place where transformation can begin. We may not be able to repair every relationship, overcome every habit, or resolve every uncertainty today. But we can make one honest acknowledgment, offer one act of kindness, or release one small grievance. Such movements may seem insignificant, yet they are the substance from which a different life gradually emerges.

Perhaps the urgency of repentance is not ultimately about running out of opportunities to satisfy a demanding God. It may be about recognizing the preciousness of a life that is already unfolding. Every day offers possibilities for love that cannot be lived retroactively, even if new opportunities for healing may yet arise. Macarius invites us to receive today not as another occasion for fear, but as a gift in which we can begin again.

### Breath Prayer

Breathing in: Let me receive the gift of today.

Breathing out: May I turn toward love without delay.
