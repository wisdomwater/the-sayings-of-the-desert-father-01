## Day 8 — Nothing Left to Defend

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen said that much of our trouble comes from forgetting who we are before God.

He remembered the Canaanite woman who came to Jesus asking for mercy. When she was given a humbling answer, she did not defend herself or demand a place of honor. She accepted the low place and continued asking in trust.

Poemen also remembered Abigail, who went out to meet David while he was burning with anger. She did not begin by blaming someone else. She stepped forward and took responsibility upon herself.

Poemen saw an image of the soul in these stories.

When we come before God without excuses, without defending our reputation, and without pretending to be better than we are, something opens.

The soul that can finally tell the truth about itself discovers that it is still loved.

### Devotional

We spend an astonishing amount of energy defending ourselves.

Sometimes the defense happens aloud. We explain what we meant, why we acted as we did, what the other person did first, or why the circumstances made our response understandable. At other times the defense takes place entirely inside us, where we quietly rehearse the evidence proving that we were reasonable after all.

There may even be truth in our explanations.

But Poemen seems interested in something deeper than determining who was technically correct. He is asking what happens when we stand before God without needing to win the case.

This is difficult because we often imagine that admitting wrongness will somehow diminish us. If I acknowledge impatience, selfishness, envy, dishonesty, or fear, perhaps I will become the accusation. So we protect ourselves from our own failures by explaining them away.

Yet the desert tradition repeatedly suggests that freedom lies in the opposite direction.

When nothing has to be hidden, nothing has to be defended.

This is not the same as accepting false accusations or allowing another person to mistreat us. Nor is it an invitation to cultivate shame. Poemen is not asking us to declare ourselves worthless. He is describing a way of standing before God in which our worth no longer depends upon maintaining an image of ourselves as innocent.

We can simply tell the truth.

Perhaps I was afraid.

Perhaps I wanted to be admired.

Perhaps I knew the kinder thing to do and chose something else.

Perhaps the other person really did hurt me, and I still used that hurt to justify hurting them in return.

These admissions do not push us away from God. Poemen suggests something almost opposite: they make intimacy possible.

A relationship built on self-protection can never become completely open. Some portion of ourselves must always remain hidden behind the defense. But when we discover that God already sees us completely, confession begins to change character.

It becomes less like entering a courtroom and more like opening a clenched hand.

There is no argument left to win.

No image left to preserve.

Only reality.

And perhaps this is why Poemen connects self-accusation with being loved by God. The love was not waiting for us to become innocent. It was waiting beneath all the effort we spent trying to appear innocent.

Once the defense falls silent, we may discover that mercy has been there all along.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I bring you what is true.*

**Breathing out:** *Hold me in your mercy.*
