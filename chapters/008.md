## Day 8 — Nothing Left to Defend

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen spoke of people in Scripture who came before God or others without defending themselves. He remembered the Canaanite woman who asked Jesus for mercy and, instead of insisting on her status, accepted the low place and continued asking in trust. He also remembered Abigail, who stepped forward during a moment of danger and took responsibility rather than beginning with blame.

Poemen saw in these stories an image of the soul before God. When we stop trying to protect our reputation, justify every action, or prove that we are better than we appear, something opens. We become capable of standing in the truth without fleeing from it.

For Poemen, this was not about humiliation for its own sake. It was about becoming free enough to stop defending an image of ourselves. Once the defenses fall away, the soul can discover that it is still held in love.

### Devotional

We spend an astonishing amount of energy defending ourselves. Sometimes the defense happens aloud, as we explain what we meant, why we acted as we did, what the other person did first, or why the circumstances made our response understandable. At other times the entire argument takes place inside us, where we quietly rehearse the evidence proving that we were reasonable after all.

There may even be truth in those explanations. Context matters, wounds matter, and another person’s actions may genuinely have contributed to what happened. But Poemen seems interested in something deeper than determining who was technically correct.

He is asking what happens when we stand before God without needing to win the case.

That is difficult because we often imagine that admitting wrongness will somehow diminish us. If I acknowledge impatience, selfishness, envy, dishonesty, or fear, perhaps I will become the accusation itself. So we protect ourselves from our own failures by explaining them away.

Yet the desert tradition repeatedly suggests that freedom lies in the opposite direction. When nothing has to be hidden, nothing has to be defended. We can allow what is true to become visible without turning the truth into a sentence against ourselves.

This is not the same as accepting false accusations or allowing others to mistreat us. Nor is it an invitation to cultivate shame or to pretend that responsibility always belongs to us. Poemen is describing something more interior: the willingness to see clearly where we have participated in what went wrong.

Perhaps I was afraid and let that fear shape my response. Perhaps I wanted to be admired more than I wanted to be honest, or I knew the kinder thing to do and chose something else. Perhaps another person really did hurt me, and I still used that wound to justify hurting them in return.

These admissions do not push us away from God. They may actually make intimacy possible, because a relationship built on self-protection can never become completely open. Some part of us always remains hidden behind the defense.

But if God already sees us completely, confession begins to change character. It becomes less like entering a courtroom and more like opening a clenched hand. There is no argument left to win and no image left to preserve.

Only reality remains.

Perhaps this is why Poemen connects self-accusation with being received by God. The love was not waiting for us to become innocent, and mercy was not waiting for us to construct the right explanation. It was already present beneath the effort we spent trying to appear blameless.

When the defense falls silent, we may discover something surprising. The truth about us is not the end of communion. It may be the place where communion finally becomes possible.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I bring you what is true.*

**Breathing out:** *Hold me in your mercy.*
