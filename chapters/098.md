## Day 98 — From Darkness Into Light

### Abba Paul the Simple

Abba Paul the Simple once visited a monastery where the brothers were gathering for worship. He was said to possess the unusual gift of perceiving the spiritual condition of those around him. As the monks entered the church, he saw their souls shining brightly, accompanied by angels who rejoiced over them. But one man appeared surrounded by darkness, with demons pulling him along while his guardian angel followed at a distance in sorrow. Paul was so troubled by the sight that he remained outside the church, weeping for the man.

When the service ended, Paul saw the same man emerging from the church, but now his appearance was radiant, and his angel walked beside him with joy. Astonished by the transformation, Paul asked what had happened. The man explained that during the service he had heard the prophet Isaiah proclaim God's invitation to turn from wrongdoing and be made clean. Moved by these words, he had resolved to abandon his former life and return to God. Paul rejoiced, recognizing that a heart sincerely turned toward goodness could be transformed in a single moment.&#x20;

### Devotional

We often carry an invisible portrait of ourselves, shaped by our memories, habits, and the judgments we have made about our lives. We remember the things we have done, the promises we have broken, and the ways we have disappointed ourselves or others. Over time, these experiences can become more than events in our history. They can begin to define who we believe we are.

Paul's story challenges the assumption that our identity is fixed by our past. The man who enters the church appears overwhelmed by darkness, yet the man who leaves is described as radiant. Nothing in the account suggests that he has suddenly accumulated years of virtue or repaired every consequence of his previous choices. What has changed is the direction of his heart.

There is something remarkable about the simplicity of this transformation. The man hears a passage from Isaiah and recognizes that its invitation is addressed to him. He does not first have to establish his worthiness or prove that he deserves another opportunity. He simply receives the possibility of a different life and responds to it.

We may wonder whether such a sudden change can truly be called transformation. After all, intentions can be sincere and still prove difficult to sustain. A person may resolve to abandon a destructive habit only to encounter the same temptation the following day. Yet the fragility of an intention does not make it meaningless, nor does the possibility of future failure erase the significance of a genuine turning.

The imagery of darkness and light can also be understood as describing our relationship with ourselves and with God. When shame becomes central to our identity, we may feel trapped beneath the weight of what we have done. We begin to see ourselves primarily through the lens of failure, as though nothing good remains visible. Grace interrupts this narrowing of vision by revealing that we are more than the worst moments of our lives.

What moves Paul most deeply is not the man's wrongdoing, but the possibility of his restoration. He weeps when he sees the man's suffering and rejoices when he sees him renewed. There is no suggestion that Paul wishes to condemn him or preserve the distinction between the righteous and the fallen. His joy comes from witnessing someone move toward life.

Perhaps this is one way to understand the transforming power of divine love. Love does not require us to pretend that our actions have caused no harm, but neither does it reduce us to the harm we have caused. It sees what is wounded without losing sight of what can be healed. It calls us toward goodness not because we have already become worthy of love, but because love has never ceased to invite us.

Paul's story invites us to consider whether we have mistaken our history for our identity. We may have spent years repeating the same patterns, carrying familiar regrets, or believing that meaningful change belongs to someone other than ourselves. Yet the possibility of turning remains present, even when we cannot see the entire road ahead. Sometimes the first movement toward a different life is simply believing that such a life is still possible.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Let me see myself through Your love.*

**Breathing out:** *Lead me from darkness into light.*
