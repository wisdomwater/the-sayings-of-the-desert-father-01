## Day 24 — The Narrow Way Within

### Abba Ammonas

Someone once asked Abba Ammonas what Jesus meant by the “narrow and hard way.” Ammonas answered that the narrow way is to govern our thoughts and to strip ourselves of our own will for the sake of God. For him, the difficult path was not first of all about what happened outside a person, but about what happened within.

He connected this with the disciples leaving everything to follow Christ. The deepest renunciation was not only possessions, place, or comfort. It was the surrender of the assumption that our own desire must always have the final word.

### Devotional

The phrase *narrow way* can easily make us imagine hardship. We may picture sacrifice, deprivation, discipline, or a life hemmed in by rules. Ammonas turns our attention somewhere more intimate.

The narrowness is inside us.

Our thoughts constantly offer interpretations of what is happening. They tell us what another person meant, what should have happened instead, what we deserve, what must be fixed, and why our preferred outcome is the reasonable one. Before long, we may no longer be responding to reality itself but to the story our mind has constructed around it.

Ammonas does not tell us to stop thinking. He tells us to bring thought under discernment. Not every thought deserves obedience simply because it appeared.

This is closely connected to what he calls surrendering our own will. The language can sound severe, especially if we imagine that spiritual maturity requires becoming passive or losing the capacity to desire. But perhaps the deeper issue is not whether we have a will, but whether we can remain free when reality does not conform to it.

Much suffering begins with a simple collision: *I want this, and this is not what is happening.* The mind immediately begins negotiating with the gap, resisting, blaming, imagining alternatives, or insisting that peace cannot return until circumstances change. Sometimes action is needed, but sometimes the struggle is with the fact that life has refused our terms.

The narrow way is the space between desire and compulsion.

We can want something deeply without declaring that we must have it. We can prefer one outcome and still remain present when another arrives. We can hold a plan sincerely without requiring the world, another person, or even God to cooperate with it.

That kind of surrender is not resignation. Resignation says that nothing matters, while surrender says that something matters deeply and yet does not own us. We can still act, speak, advocate, choose, and hope without fastening our peace to the outcome.

This may be why Ammonas joins thought and will together. Thoughts often become the advocates of self-will, building elaborate cases for why reality should yield to us. If we do not watch them, they can make every inconvenience into an injustice and every disappointment into a personal offense.

Humility creates another possibility.

We can notice the thought and let it remain a thought. We can feel the desire and let it remain a desire. Between the first movement of the heart and the action that follows, a small space of freedom begins to open.

Perhaps this is the narrowness Ammonas is describing. It is not a cramped life, but a precise passage through the places where ego usually spreads itself wide. We travel lighter when we no longer need every thought to be true or every preference to become reality.

The disciples left nets, boats, homes, and familiar lives to follow Christ. Most of us may never be asked to leave in the same way. But every day offers smaller invitations to release the thing we were certain had to happen.

The narrow way may appear in a conversation that does not go as planned, a delayed answer, a changed schedule, a criticism, an unanswered desire, or a future we cannot control. In those moments, the question is not whether we have a will. It is whether we can hold it with open hands.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I release what I cannot control.*

**Breathing out:** *Lead me in the way of freedom.*
