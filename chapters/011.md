## Day 11 — Return to the Water

### Abba Anthony

Abba Anthony said that just as fish cannot remain out of water for long and still live, monks cannot remain away from their cells indefinitely without losing something inwardly. The more they drift into noise, conversation, and constant outward movement, the more their inner watchfulness begins to fade.

So Anthony urged them to return to their cells as a fish returns to the sea. The cell was not merely a room, but the place where attention could settle again. It was where they remembered how to become inwardly alive.

### Devotional

Anthony’s image is severe, but it is also wonderfully simple. A fish does not return to water because water is an interesting spiritual practice; it returns because water is the environment in which it can live. Out of water, the fish may survive for a while, but something essential is being lost with every passing moment.

Anthony believed stillness could become something like that for the human soul. His monks had literal cells in the Egyptian desert, places of prayer, work, and quiet to which they continually returned. Most of us do not live that way, but the deeper meaning of the cell remains.

There are places and practices in which we become able to hear ourselves again. We may discover this sitting quietly before anyone else wakes, walking without headphones, praying in a familiar chair, tending a garden, or simply putting the phone down and allowing nothing new to enter for a while. The point is not the specific setting, but the return to a space where attention is no longer being constantly pulled outward.

Anthony’s image can be misunderstood if we hear it as a rejection of other people or of ordinary life. The world is not poison, and relationship is not the enemy of contemplation. The danger is that constant outward movement can gradually make us strangers to our own interior life.

We become accustomed to stimulation so quickly. One conversation ends and another begins, one article leads to another, one notification becomes ten, and every empty space is filled before we have had time to notice that it was empty. Eventually, silence itself can begin to feel uncomfortable.

Then we may discover that we have been living outside the water for quite some time. We are functioning, working, answering, reacting, and moving, but something in us has become thin. The inner life has not disappeared, but we have lost contact with it.

The cell is where we return.

It may be an actual place, but eventually it becomes something inward as well. It is the willingness to stop following every thought, every urgency, and every invitation to react long enough for attention to settle again.

At first, nothing dramatic may happen. We may simply notice that we are tired, restless, lonely, or afraid, or that we have been carrying an emotion we were too busy to feel. Stillness does not always make us peaceful immediately; sometimes it first reveals how far from peace we have wandered.

But even that recognition is a form of return. We do not need to condemn ourselves for distraction, just as a fish does not need to feel guilty for leaving the sea. We only need to remember where life becomes deeper again.

Perhaps this is what Anthony is offering us. Not an escape from the world, but a rhythm of returning often enough that the world does not completely scatter us. We step back into the water, breathe again, and remember the depth from which we want to live.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Draw me back to stillness.*

**Breathing out:** *Let me rest in you.*
