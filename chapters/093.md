## Day 93 — The Grace of Beginning Again

### Abba John the Dwarf

A young woman named Paesia lost her parents and inherited their home. Wanting to honor God, she opened her house to the monks of Scetis, offering them hospitality and whatever provisions she could afford. Over time, her resources were exhausted, and she fell into poverty. Others took advantage of her vulnerability, and she eventually abandoned her former way of life and became involved in prostitution. When the monks learned what had happened, they were deeply saddened and sent Abba John the Dwarf to visit her.

John found Paesia and spoke with her about the life she had left behind. As he wept for her, she asked whether repentance was still possible, and he assured her that it was. She immediately agreed to leave with him, abandoning her house and following him into the desert. That night, as she slept, John saw a vision of angels carrying her soul toward heaven, and he discovered that she had died. He then received the assurance that a single hour of wholehearted repentance could be worth more than many years of halfhearted religious effort.

### Devotional

We sometimes imagine that the direction of a life can only be changed through years of disciplined effort. Habits take time to form, wounds take time to heal, and meaningful transformation rarely happens overnight. When we look at the distance between who we are and who we hope to become, the journey can seem overwhelming. Paesia's story introduces a surprising possibility: a single moment of openness can change the direction of an entire life.

There is something especially moving about the way her story begins. Paesia was not someone who had always lived carelessly or disregarded the needs of others. She had opened her home in generosity, given what she possessed, and eventually found herself vulnerable when those resources were gone. Her suffering reminds us that the circumstances surrounding a person's choices are often more complicated than the judgments others might make about them.

The monks themselves appear to recognize this complexity. They remember the kindness Paesia once showed them and respond to her situation by sending someone to help. John does not arrive merely to announce that she has failed or to demand an accounting of her mistakes. His tears reveal genuine sorrow over what has happened to someone whose life still matters deeply.

Perhaps the most important moment comes when Paesia asks whether repentance remains possible. Beneath her question lies a fear that many people know intimately: Have I gone too far to return? We may believe that certain failures have permanently altered our identity or placed us beyond the reach of grace. John answers her with a simple assurance that the way remains open.

Repentance, in its deepest sense, is not merely feeling badly about what we have done. It is a turning of the heart, a reorientation toward the life and love from which we have become estranged. This turning may be accompanied by regret, grief, or an awareness of harm, but it need not be defined by endless self-condemnation. Its purpose is not to imprison us in the past, but to open a different future.

The story's ending is striking because Paesia has no time to demonstrate a long record of changed behavior. There are no years of spiritual discipline through which she proves the sincerity of her repentance. She simply turns, and the story tells us that her turning is received. The vision challenges the assumption that grace must be earned through an accumulation of religious accomplishments.

This does not mean that lasting transformation ordinarily requires no patience or sustained effort. Most of us will need to return to our intentions repeatedly, learning new ways of living through countless small decisions. Yet the value of those efforts does not depend upon establishing an impressive spiritual record. Their meaning begins with the sincerity of the heart that turns toward the good.

Paesia's story invites us to release the belief that our past must determine our future. We may carry consequences, unfinished healing, and habits that cannot be changed in a single afternoon. But none of these realities makes the possibility of beginning again meaningless. Wherever we find ourselves today, the next movement of the heart can still be toward love.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *The way remains open.*

**Breathing out:** *I turn again toward Your love.*
