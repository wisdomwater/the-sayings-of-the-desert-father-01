# Day 41 — The Good of My Brother

### Abba Agathon

Abba Agathon said that he had never made the formal offerings of love that others might have expected. Instead, he understood the ordinary exchange of giving and receiving as his offering. For him, seeking the good of his brother was itself a sacrifice placed before God.

Agathon shifts the center of devotion away from display and toward relationship. What mattered was not whether his love looked impressive from the outside, but whether another person was genuinely helped by it. He saw care for his brother as something holy enough to belong on the altar.

### Devotional

We often imagine sacrifice in dramatic terms. We think of giving up something costly, making a difficult commitment, or performing some act that clearly demonstrates devotion. Agathon brings the idea much closer to ordinary life.

He places sacrifice inside relationship. The offering is not only what leaves our hands, but what happens to another person because of our presence. If they are strengthened, encouraged, fed, heard, welcomed, or relieved of some burden, something sacred has taken place. Their good becomes part of our worship.

This changes how we think about spiritual practice. Prayer, fasting, study, silence, and generosity all matter, but Agathon refuses to let them remain detached from love. If our practices make us more devoted in appearance while making us less attentive to the person beside us, something has gone wrong. The movement toward God should deepen our capacity to care.

There is also humility in Agathon’s emphasis on giving and receiving. Love is not only what we provide for others. Sometimes hospitality means allowing another person to give something to us, accepting their kindness without needing to remain the strong or self-sufficient one. Mutuality can be as holy as generosity.

This can be difficult for people who are more comfortable giving than receiving. To receive is to admit that we also have needs. It asks us to let someone else contribute, help, encourage, or carry part of the weight. Agathon seems to see this exchange itself as part of love.

The good of another person can therefore become a kind of compass. When we are uncertain about what love requires, we can ask whether our action is actually serving their good. That does not mean pleasing them in every circumstance, because what is good for someone is not always what they immediately want. It means taking their well-being seriously enough to let it shape our response.

Sometimes this will be simple. We may listen without interrupting, make room in a crowded schedule, share what we have, or offer encouragement when someone is discouraged. None of these acts may feel heroic, yet they can become places where love takes visible form. Agathon invites us to notice the sacredness of such ordinary exchanges.

At other times, seeking another person’s good may require restraint. We may need to resist the urge to correct, control, rescue, or make ourselves central to their story. Love can involve giving space, allowing another person to choose, or refusing to make their struggle about our own need to feel useful. Their good, not our importance, remains the measure.

This is what makes Agathon’s saying demanding. It is easier to perform a recognizable religious act than to continually ask what love requires in a particular relationship. Ritual can often be completed, while another person remains unpredictable, complicated, and impossible to manage. Relationship asks more of our attention.

Yet Agathon sees that very difficulty as the place of offering. The altar is not far away from daily life. It appears wherever one person becomes willing to seek the good of another without needing recognition in return. Love becomes worship when another person’s flourishing matters enough to shape what we do.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Teach me to seek another’s good.*

**Breathing out:** *Let my love become an offering.*
