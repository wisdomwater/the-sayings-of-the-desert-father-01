## Day 30 — Better to Return

### Abba Sarmatas

Abba Sarmatas said that he preferred a person who had sinned, recognized what they had done, and repented to someone who had not sinned and considered themselves righteous. His comparison sounds strange at first because surely avoiding the wrong would be better than committing it. Yet Sarmatas is looking beyond the outward act to what is happening in the heart.

Failure can leave a person open. Success can sometimes close them.

The sinner in Sarmatas’s saying still knows how to turn around. The righteous person is in greater danger if goodness has become proof of superiority. One has fallen and knows it, while the other may be standing upright without realizing how far the heart has wandered.

### Devotional

There is a kind of spiritual success that can become dangerous precisely because it feels like success. We overcome a habit, resist a temptation, become more disciplined, or learn to respond better than we once did. What begins as gratitude can slowly become confidence in ourselves.

Then comparison enters.

We notice that another person is still struggling with something we have learned to manage. Their choices begin to seem obvious, weak, or avoidable to us. Without intending to, we cross a subtle boundary from gratitude into judgment.

Sarmatas would rather see the person who has fallen and returned.

This does not mean that sin is good or that failure should be pursued for its spiritual benefits. Harm remains harm, and some actions leave consequences that repentance does not simply erase. Sarmatas is speaking about what failure can reveal when we allow it to tell the truth.

The person who falls may discover their dependence.

They may learn that good intentions are not enough, that temptation can be stronger than expected, and that the distance between themselves and the person they once judged is smaller than they imagined. Failure can break open the illusion of self-sufficiency.

Repentance begins there.

It is more than feeling bad about what happened. Repentance is the willingness to turn, to come back into truth, and to choose again. It keeps the heart moving rather than freezing it inside either failure or pride.

The apparently righteous person faces a different danger. If their behavior has become evidence that they are fundamentally better than others, repentance can become almost impossible. They no longer know where to return from because they believe they have already arrived.

This is one of pride’s strangest effects.

It can turn virtue into insulation. Correction cannot enter because we already know we are right, compassion weakens because other people should simply do what we did, and grace becomes something we acknowledge theologically while quietly imagining that we no longer need much of it ourselves.

The sinner who returns may actually stand on safer ground.

They know what mercy feels like. They remember the distance between aspiration and action, and they are less surprised by weakness when they see it in another person. Their wound can become a doorway into compassion rather than a permanent identity.

There is something deeply Christian in this movement. Again and again, Jesus seems less troubled by obvious sinners who know their need than by those whose righteousness has made them incapable of recognizing it. The danger is not goodness, but goodness turned into self-possession.

Humility therefore does not ask us to distrust every good thing growing within us. It asks us to receive goodness as gift rather than proof. We can rejoice in growth without turning it into a ranking system.

Perhaps the real question is not whether we have fallen.

All of us will fail in ways large and small. The more important question is whether we still know how to return when truth reveals the distance between who we are and who we hoped to be. A heart that can return remains alive.

Sarmatas leaves us with an unexpected measure of spiritual maturity. It may not be the person who never appears to stumble, but the person who has become very practiced at turning back toward God. Humility does not guarantee that we will never fall; it keeps the road home open when we do.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Keep my heart open to truth.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me always to return.*
