# Day 54 — Not Every Battle Is Fought the Same Way

### Abba Joseph of Panephysis

Abba Poemen asked Abba Joseph what he should do when the passions approached him. Joseph told him to let them enter and then struggle against them. Later, Poemen heard that Joseph had given another brother very different advice, telling him not to let the passions enter at all but to cut them off immediately.

Poemen returned and asked why Joseph had spoken differently to the two of them. Joseph explained that he had spoken to Poemen as he would have spoken to himself, because wrestling with the passions could strengthen him. But some people would not benefit from that struggle and needed to turn away before the passion gained any ground.

### Devotional

We often want one technique that will work for every temptation. Resist the thought, sit with the feeling, distract yourself, pray, confront it, ignore it, or let it pass. Joseph refuses to give us a formula that simple.

His two answers initially appear contradictory. One person is told to let the passion come close enough to struggle with it, while another is told not to give it even the smallest opening. The difference lies not in the passion itself but in the person facing it.

Joseph understands that spiritual maturity requires knowing our own capacity. A struggle that strengthens one person may overwhelm another. Wisdom therefore asks not only what is theoretically possible, but what actually leads this particular person toward freedom.

Sometimes facing a desire directly can teach us something important. We notice the craving, fear, anger, or fantasy without immediately obeying it, and we discover that it can be endured. By staying present, we may learn that the feeling rises, changes, and eventually loosens its hold.

At other times, staying near the temptation is not courageous but unwise. We already know the pattern well enough to recognize where it leads. In those moments, freedom may mean turning away early rather than testing how long we can remain near the edge.

This makes self-knowledge essential. We need to learn which desires become weaker when observed and which become stronger through attention. We need to recognize the situations in which curiosity helps us and the situations in which curiosity becomes negotiation. The same internal movement may require a different response at a different stage of life.

Joseph also warns us against comparing our strategy with someone else's. Another person may be able to remain near something that would quickly pull us off course. Their freedom does not make our caution cowardice, and our restraint does not make their approach reckless.

There is humility in accepting our actual limits. We may want to prove that we are strong enough to face a particular temptation directly. Yet sometimes the wiser act is to remove the opportunity, leave the situation, change the environment, or interrupt the thought before it develops. Freedom does not require demonstrating that we could have resisted.

The reverse can also be true. We may avoid every uncomfortable desire so quickly that we never discover what lies underneath it. A recurring temptation may carry information about loneliness, anger, grief, fear, exhaustion, or some other unmet need. There are times when turning toward the feeling with attention allows healing that simple avoidance cannot provide.

Discernment therefore asks a more personal question than simply, “What is the rule?” It asks what kind of response produces greater freedom, honesty, and love in this moment. Sometimes the answer will be to stay and struggle, and sometimes it will be to leave immediately. The wisdom lies in knowing which battle is actually ours to fight.

Joseph’s teaching leaves room for growth. A person who once needed to flee quickly may later become able to remain present without being carried away. Someone who once benefited from wrestling may discover that repeated exposure is now only exhausting them. The spiritual life is living enough to require continued attention.

Perhaps maturity is not having one perfect strategy for desire. It is learning to recognize ourselves truthfully and respond without pride. We become less interested in proving our strength and more interested in choosing the path that leads toward freedom.
