## Day 12 — What We Keep Near

### Abba Arsenius

Abba Arsenius said:

“If we seek God, God will make himself known to us.

And if we hold fast to God, God will remain close.”

The spiritual life begins with attention.

We turn toward what we desire to know.

And what we continue to attend to gradually becomes the place where we live.

### Devotional

There is a difference between occasionally thinking about God and seeking God. Seeking carries movement within it. Something in us has become interested enough to turn, to look, to listen, perhaps even to wait when nothing immediately appears.

Arsenius trusted that this movement matters. If we seek God, he says, God will reveal himself. That does not necessarily mean visions, certainty, or dramatic spiritual experiences. Often the revelation is much quieter. We begin noticing something we previously overlooked: a stillness underneath our anxiety, compassion where resentment once dominated, beauty in something ordinary, or the strange sense that we are not as alone as we imagined.

Attention changes what becomes visible. This is true in almost every part of life. A birdwatcher notices movements in trees that the rest of us walk past. A musician hears patterns inside a song that another person barely registers. Someone who loves another person begins recognizing tiny shifts in expression and tone.

We learn to perceive what we continually attend to. Perhaps this is part of what Arsenius means by seeking God. God may not be absent so much as unnoticed.

Our attention is finite, and the world makes countless claims upon it. Anxiety asks us to rehearse what might go wrong. Desire invites us to imagine what we do not yet have. Resentment repeatedly brings another person's offense before us. Screens offer an endless procession of things worth noticing for a few seconds and forgetting almost immediately.

None of this makes us bad. It simply means that attention has a direction. And whatever repeatedly receives our attention begins to occupy our interior world.

Arsenius adds a second movement: if we keep God, God remains near. This is not about preventing God from leaving, as though divine presence depends upon our concentration. Perhaps it is more about maintaining our availability to what is already present.

We notice. Then we remain. We return when distracted. We allow a little silence to stay unfilled. We remember God while washing dishes, walking down a hallway, sitting at a desk, or lying awake at night. Over time, remembrance can become less like an activity we perform and more like a background awareness in which the rest of life takes place.

The desert elders sometimes called this watchfulness. Not straining. Not constantly producing religious thoughts. Simply remembering where we are. Perhaps contemplation begins there. We seek until we notice. We notice until presence becomes familiar. And slowly we discover that the One we were looking for has been nearer than our own breath.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I seek your presence.*

**Breathing out:** *Remain near to me.*
