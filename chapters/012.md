## Day 12 — What We Keep Near

### Abba Arsenius

Abba Arsenius said that if we seek God, God will make himself known to us. And if we hold fast to God, God will remain close. The spiritual life begins with attention, because we gradually learn to perceive what we repeatedly turn toward.

Seeking is more than occasionally thinking about God. It carries desire, movement, and a willingness to wait when nothing immediately appears. Arsenius trusted that this movement of the heart matters.

### Devotional

There is a difference between thinking about God and seeking God. Seeking means that something within us has become interested enough to turn, to listen, and to remain open even when certainty does not come quickly. It is less like solving a problem and more like becoming receptive.

Arsenius says that if we seek God, God will reveal himself to us. That does not necessarily mean visions, dramatic experiences, or sudden certainty. Often the revelation is quieter: a stillness beneath our anxiety, compassion where resentment once dominated, beauty in something ordinary, or the strange sense that we are not as alone as we imagined.

Attention changes what becomes visible.

This is true in almost every part of life. A musician hears patterns inside a song that someone else barely notices, while a birdwatcher sees movement in a tree most people would pass without a glance. Someone who loves another person begins to recognize small changes in expression, posture, and tone.

We learn to perceive what we continually attend to.

Perhaps seeking God works in a similar way. God may not be absent so much as unnoticed, obscured by the sheer number of things competing for our attention. The problem is not always that God has withdrawn, but that we have become scattered.

Our attention is finite, and the world makes endless claims upon it. Anxiety asks us to rehearse what might go wrong, desire invites us to imagine what we do not yet have, and resentment repeatedly brings another person’s offense before us. Screens offer an almost infinite procession of things worth noticing for a few seconds and then forgetting.

None of this makes us bad. It simply means that attention has a direction, and whatever repeatedly receives that attention begins to occupy our interior world. What we keep near eventually begins to shape the atmosphere in which we live.

Arsenius adds a second movement: if we hold fast to God, God remains near. This is not about preventing God from leaving, as though divine presence depends upon our concentration. Perhaps it is more about maintaining our availability to the Presence that is already here.

We notice, and then we remain. We return when distracted, allow a little silence to stay unfilled, and remember God while washing dishes, walking down a hallway, sitting at a desk, or lying awake at night. Over time, remembrance can become less like an activity we perform and more like a quiet background awareness within which the rest of life unfolds.

The desert elders often called this watchfulness. It is not constant strain or the production of religious thoughts, but a kind of remembering where we are. We begin to sense that the ordinary moment is already taking place within something larger than ourselves.

Perhaps contemplation begins there. We seek until we notice, and we notice until presence becomes familiar. Slowly, we discover that the One we were looking for has been nearer than our own breath.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I seek your presence.*

**Breathing out:** *Remain near to me.*
