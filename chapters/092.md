## Day 92 — Beyond Our Own Judgment

### Abba Agathon

Abba Agathon devoted his life to serving God and loving those around him. When traveling by boat, he was the first to take up an oar, and when visitors arrived, he personally prepared their table. Yet as he approached death, he lay for three days with his eyes open, seemingly absorbed in something beyond the room. When the brothers asked where his thoughts had gone, he told them that he was standing before the judgment of God.

The brothers wondered how someone who had lived so faithfully could still feel uncertain. Agathon replied that he had done everything within his power to follow God's commandments, but he could not know whether his efforts had truly pleased God. Human judgments, even our judgments about ourselves, are not the same as God's. When the brothers tried to question him further, he asked for silence, and the account tells us that he departed with joy, like someone greeting beloved friends.

### Devotional

We spend much of our lives trying to determine whether we have done enough. We measure our accomplishments, examine our failures, and compare ourselves with the people we hoped to become. Even when our efforts have been sincere, there is often a lingering question about whether they have amounted to something worthwhile. We long for some final assurance that our lives have been good.

Agathon's uncertainty may seem surprising, especially after a lifetime of devotion. He had practiced the virtues he taught, served others generously, and pursued God with remarkable dedication. Yet he did not consider himself qualified to deliver the final judgment on his own life. He understood that a human perspective, even one shaped by years of contemplation, remains incomplete.

There is something liberating about recognizing the limits of our own judgment. We cannot see every consequence of our actions or understand all the ways our presence has affected others. A kindness we barely remember may have changed someone's life, while an accomplishment we cherish may have mattered less than we imagined. Our ability to evaluate ourselves is necessarily constrained by what we can see.

This does not mean that our choices are unimportant or that honest self-examination has no value. Agathon's life was marked by extraordinary attention to the way he treated others. He took responsibility for his actions without assuming that he could calculate their ultimate worth. His faithfulness was expressed in the ordinary work of rowing a boat, preparing a meal, and caring for those who came to him.

We sometimes approach the spiritual life as though it were an account that must eventually be balanced. We collect our good deeds, regret our failures, and hope that the final reckoning will come out in our favor. But love does not operate like an accounting system, and a life cannot be reduced to a ledger of successes and mistakes. The deepest meaning of our existence may lie beyond anything we can adequately measure.

The distinction between human judgment and divine judgment can initially sound frightening. We may imagine God as a more exacting evaluator, discovering faults that escaped our own scrutiny. Yet if God is love, divine judgment might also reveal goodness we have overlooked and possibilities for healing we could never have imagined. To be fully known need not mean being reduced to our worst moments.

Perhaps this is why the ending of Agathon's story is so remarkable. He begins with uncertainty, but the account concludes with joy, as though he were greeting dear friends. We are not told exactly what changed within him during those final moments. We are simply given an image of someone who had stopped explaining himself and was ready to encounter the mystery before him.

Agathon invites us to live faithfully without demanding that we become the final authorities on our own worth. We can examine our lives, acknowledge our failures, and celebrate whatever goodness has taken root within us. But eventually, we must release the desire to know precisely what our lives have meant. We can entrust the whole unfinished story to a love whose understanding reaches further than our own.

### Breath Prayer

Breathing in: I release the need to measure my worth.

Breathing out: I entrust my life to Your love.
