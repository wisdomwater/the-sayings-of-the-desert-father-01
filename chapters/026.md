## Day 26 — What Strength Cannot Do

### Amma Theodora

Amma Theodora said that asceticism, vigils, and suffering are not enough to save a person. She told of an anchorite who could drive away demons and asked them what power defeated them. He wondered whether it was fasting, sleeplessness, or withdrawal into the desert.

The demons dismissed each answer. They did not eat, they did not sleep, and they themselves lived in desolate places. When the anchorite finally asked what could overcome them, they answered that only humility could do it.

Theodora’s point was not that discipline was useless. It was that discipline without humility could leave the deepest problem untouched. A person might become extraordinarily strong and still remain centered on the self.

### Devotional

We are naturally impressed by visible effort. The person who fasts, works harder, sleeps less, sacrifices more, or endures greater difficulty seems serious in a way we can recognize. Strength gives us something measurable to admire.

Theodora is suspicious of that measurement.

Her story imagines even the demons as capable of austerity. They do not eat or sleep, and they are perfectly capable of inhabiting the wilderness. If spiritual maturity were simply a contest of deprivation, they would be formidable ascetics.

Something else must matter.

Humility reaches a place that force cannot. Discipline can restrain behavior, strengthen attention, and teach endurance, but it can also become another way of constructing an impressive self. We may begin by trying to become free and end by becoming proud of how disciplined we are.

That danger is subtle because the practices themselves may be good. Prayer, fasting, simplicity, study, service, and self-restraint can all deepen us. Yet any of them can be quietly recruited into the project of becoming someone we can admire.

We may start comparing.

We notice who prays less, who lacks discipline, who gives in more easily, or who seems less committed. The very practice meant to loosen the ego begins feeding it.

Theodora places humility beneath the whole structure.

Humility remembers that every capacity we have is received. Our discipline depends on health, circumstance, temperament, teachers, grace, and countless forms of support we did not create for ourselves. Even the desire to seek God arrived before we could claim credit for it.

This does not diminish effort. It simply places effort inside gratitude.

Humility also keeps us tender toward weakness. When we know how dependent we are, another person’s struggle becomes harder to despise. We may still recognize destructive choices, but we are less tempted to imagine that our own strength places us in a different category of human being.

Perhaps this is what the demons in Theodora’s story cannot tolerate. Pride survives by separation: higher and lower, worthy and unworthy, strong and weak, righteous and fallen. Humility dissolves the hierarchy by returning everyone to the same ground.

We are creatures.

We need mercy.

We do not sustain ourselves.

That realization can reach places ascetic achievement cannot. A person may conquer appetite and still be enslaved to admiration, endure suffering and still demand recognition for it, or renounce possessions while remaining fiercely attached to being seen as holy. Humility exposes the attachment beneath the achievement.

Theodora is not asking us to abandon discipline. She is asking what our discipline is making us into. If the practice is real, it should gradually make us less interested in superiority and more available to love.

Perhaps the strongest person in the room is not the one who can endure the most. It may be the one who no longer needs strength to make them greater than anyone else. Humility accomplishes what force alone never can because it releases the self from the exhausting work of becoming impressive.
