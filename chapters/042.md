# Day 42 — To Share Another’s Burden

### Abba Agathon

Abba Agathon said that if he met a person with leprosy, he would gladly give that person his own healthy body and take the diseased one in exchange. The saying describes this willingness as perfect charity. Agathon imagines love not merely as sympathy, but as the desire to enter fully into another person’s suffering.

His words are deliberately radical. Most of us instinctively want to help suffering from a position of safety, offering what we can while remaining outside the pain itself. Agathon pushes love toward a deeper identification with the person who suffers.

### Devotional

There is a kind of compassion that remains at a distance. We see another person hurting, recognize that their pain is real, and perhaps offer comfort or assistance. Yet some part of us remains relieved that the suffering belongs to them and not to us.

Agathon challenges that distance. He does not merely say that he would help the sick person or provide what was needed. He imagines exchanging places with them, receiving into himself the very condition from which he wishes them to be freed. His image asks how far love is willing to move toward another person’s burden.

We may never be asked to make such a literal exchange. Still, there are many smaller ways in which love invites us to share what another person is carrying. We give time when someone needs to be heard, absorb inconvenience when someone cannot manage alone, or remain present when there is nothing useful left to say. Compassion becomes more costly when it begins to alter our own comfort.

This is where Agathon’s saying becomes difficult. It is easy to care in ways that fit neatly into our schedule, preserve our emotional distance, and leave our own plans mostly untouched. The suffering of another person becomes more demanding when it interrupts us or asks us to remain beside something we cannot quickly solve. Love then becomes less about fixing pain and more about refusing to abandon the one who bears it.

There is also a danger in romanticizing this kind of self-giving. Agathon is not teaching us to erase ourselves, ignore healthy boundaries, or seek suffering for its own sake. His image is about the direction of love, not about cultivating harm. Love moves toward the vulnerable rather than protecting itself from every cost.

We see something similar in the Christian image of incarnation. Christ does not remain distant from human frailty but enters it, sharing hunger, grief, rejection, weariness, pain, and death. Divine compassion takes flesh within the conditions it seeks to heal. Agathon’s saying echoes that movement by imagining love as participation rather than observation.

This changes the question we bring to another person’s suffering. Instead of asking only what advice might help or what problem might be solved, we can ask what portion of this burden we are able to carry with them. Sometimes that means practical help, while at other times it means attention, patience, advocacy, or quiet presence. The answer will differ with each relationship and circumstance.

Sharing another person’s burden also requires humility. We cannot know exactly what another person feels simply because we have suffered something similar. Their pain remains their own, shaped by a history and interior world we cannot fully enter. Love comes near without pretending complete understanding.

Agathon’s image is powerful because it refuses to make suffering abstract. The person with leprosy is not a lesson, a problem, or a category of need. He is another human being whose pain matters enough that Agathon wishes he could relieve it even at personal cost. Compassion begins when the person becomes more important to us than our distance from their suffering.

Most love will be lived on a smaller scale than Agathon’s imagined exchange. We carry groceries, answer the late call, sit beside a hospital bed, make room for grief, forgive an inconvenience, or rearrange a plan because another person needs us. These ordinary acts become ways of saying that their burden does not have to remain entirely theirs.

Perhaps perfect charity is not measured by how intensely we feel another person’s pain. It may be revealed in how willing we are to let their need touch our own life. Agathon invites us toward a love that comes close enough to bear some of the weight.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Draw me near to those who suffer.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to help carry the burden.*
