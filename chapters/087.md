## Day 87 — Escaping the Pedestal

### Abba Simon

A governor once came looking for Abba Simon because he had heard of the old man’s reputation. When Simon learned that the visitor was approaching, he put on his work belt and went out to prune a palm tree. The governor’s party saw him and asked where the famous solitary lived. Simon simply told them there was no such person there, and they went away.

Simon does not merely decline praise after receiving it. He deliberately steps out of the role others are preparing for him. Rather than become the holy man they have imagined, he makes himself look ordinary enough to be overlooked. He protects his inner freedom by refusing the pedestal before anyone can place him on it.

### Devotional

Admiration can feel harmless because it comes wrapped in affection and respect. Yet being admired can slowly change the way we see ourselves. We may begin performing the person others believe us to be.

Spiritual reputation can be especially dangerous. If people think we are wise, peaceful, compassionate, or unusually close to God, we may feel pressure to preserve that image. The desire to appear transformed can quietly replace the harder work of actually being transformed.

Simon chooses obscurity instead. He does not seem interested in proving that the governor’s admiration is justified. He would rather be mistaken for an ordinary laborer than become dependent on being recognized as holy.

There is freedom in being willing to disappoint an image. We do not always have to live up to other people’s projections, especially when those projections make us less honest. Sometimes humility means allowing ourselves to appear less impressive than we could.

This does not require hiding every gift or refusing every compliment. Our abilities can serve others, and gratitude is healthier than pretending we have nothing to offer. The danger comes when our identity begins to require recognition.

A pedestal also creates distance. Once someone is treated as unusually spiritual, their ordinary humanity can become difficult to acknowledge. Weaknesses must be concealed, mistakes become threatening, and relationships can shift from mutuality toward performance.

Simon returns himself to the ordinary world of work. He is not a spiritual object to be admired from a distance, but a man pruning a tree. His holiness, if it exists, does not need the governor’s recognition in order to remain real.

The story invites us to notice where reputation has become something we must protect. We can receive appreciation without building a home inside it, and we can let ourselves be ordinary without feeling diminished. Freedom grows when being known by God matters more than being thought impressive by others.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Free me from the need to impress.*

**Breathing out:** *Let me rest in being known.*
