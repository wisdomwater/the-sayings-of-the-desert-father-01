## Day 2 — Until the Last Breath

### Abba Anthony the Great

Abba Anthony once spoke with Abba Poemen about the great work of the spiritual life. He told him to keep his own failures before God rather than continually placing responsibility somewhere else. There would always be explanations available, circumstances to point toward, or another person whose actions seemed to justify his own.

Anthony urged him instead to remain willing to see himself truthfully before God. At the same time, he warned Poemen not to imagine that temptation would someday disappear entirely. The struggle, he said, would remain with him until his final breath.

### Devotional

There is something almost disappointing about Anthony's words. We may hope that after enough prayer, enough wisdom, and enough years of practice, we will eventually arrive somewhere beyond temptation. Surely maturity should mean that the old desires stop appearing, the old fears lose their voices, and the familiar weaknesses finally cease knocking at the door.

Anthony offers no such promise. He seems to imagine the spiritual life not as reaching a place where struggle disappears, but as learning how to meet struggle differently. Temptation may remain, but we no longer have to be surprised by its arrival.

The return of an old thought does not mean that everything we have practiced has failed. The sudden appearance of anger, desire, fear, envy, resentment, or craving does not erase whatever growth has already taken place. Perhaps maturity is revealed precisely in our growing ability to notice what has arisen, receive the moment honestly, and choose again.

Anthony joins this expectation of struggle with another practice: taking responsibility for our own failures before God. That language can sound harsh if we hear it through the lens of shame, as though spiritual maturity means continually accusing ourselves. But Anthony is not asking us to declare ourselves worthless; he is asking us to stop spending so much energy proving that our failures ultimately belong to someone else.

There is freedom in this kind of honesty. We can acknowledge that another person hurt us without pretending that we had no choice in how we responded. We can recognize the pressures, wounds, and circumstances that made a particular choice more difficult without using them to avoid seeing our own participation.

Before God, there is no need for either prosecution or defense. We do not need to construct a case against ourselves, nor do we need to build an argument for our innocence. There is simply the truth of what happened, seen clearly and held within mercy.

Perhaps Anthony's two teachings belong together more closely than they first appear. If we expect temptation to disappear, every recurrence becomes evidence that something must be wrong with us. We become ashamed that we are still struggling with something we thought we should have conquered years ago.

Shame then makes honesty more difficult. We begin hiding precisely what needs to be brought into the light, because admitting the struggle feels like admitting that we have failed spiritually. Anthony offers another way: expect the struggle, and when you stumble, tell the truth and return.

The goal is not to become a person who never again feels the pull of anger, fear, desire, resentment, envy, or an old habit. The goal is to become increasingly free in their presence, able to feel the movement without automatically becoming its servant. Temptation may knock at the door until our last breath, but knocking is not the same thing as entering.

There may never be a final morning when we wake beyond the need to choose. Yet perhaps this is not bad news, because it means that every moment remains alive with possibility. Until the last breath, there is another opportunity to notice what is happening, another opportunity to tell the truth, and another opportunity to return.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I meet this moment honestly.*

**Breathing out:** *God, help me choose again.*
