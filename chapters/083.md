## Day 83 — What Cannot Be Taken

### Abba Macarius the Great

Abba Macarius once discovered a man loading Macarius’s possessions onto a pack animal to steal them. Instead of confronting him, Macarius approached as though he were a stranger and helped him arrange the load. When everything was ready, he let the man depart in peace. He remembered that we enter the world carrying nothing and ultimately leave it the same way.

Macarius does not pretend that the possessions were never his or that theft suddenly became good. What is remarkable is how little power the loss seems to have over his inner life. The thief can take what belongs to Macarius without taking Macarius’s peace with it. His freedom rests somewhere deeper than what can be carried away.

### Devotional

Possessions are useful, necessary, and often precious to us. They can represent years of labor, memories, security, and the ability to care for ourselves and others. Macarius does not require us to pretend that losing such things would be painless.

His response instead asks where ownership ends and identity begins. Something can belong to us without becoming part of who we are. When those two become confused, every threat to what we possess begins to feel like a threat to the self.

We can become possessed by things without noticing it. We organize our lives around protecting them, worry continually about losing them, and measure safety by how firmly they remain under our control. The object may sit quietly in the room while our attention circles around it.

Macarius demonstrates another kind of relationship to material life. He can use what he has without demanding that it remain his forever. When loss arrives, painful as it may be, he does not allow it to determine the condition of his heart.

This does not mean we should cooperate with theft or ignore reasonable boundaries. Protecting a home, caring for a family, and responding appropriately to wrongdoing are part of ordinary responsibility. The deeper question is what happens within us when something we value can no longer be kept.

Loss reveals where we have located our security. Sometimes we discover that an object, role, amount of money, reputation, or expectation was carrying far more of our identity than we realized. Its disappearance exposes the attachment that possession had concealed.

Detachment is therefore not indifference. It is the freedom to cherish what has been given without insisting that it must remain forever. Gratitude receives things as gifts, while grasping tries to turn gifts into guarantees.

Macarius stands beside the departing thief with less than he had before, yet something essential remains untouched. His possessions are leaving, but his freedom is not leaving with them. The practice is to hold what we have with enough openness that, when something must go, our deepest life does not go with it.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Teach me to receive with gratitude.*
