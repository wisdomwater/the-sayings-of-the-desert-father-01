## Day 74 — When It Is Time to Leave

### Abba Agathon

Abba Agathon and his disciples spent a long time building a cell together. Soon after they moved in, Agathon noticed something about the place that seemed harmful to the life they were trying to cultivate. He told the brothers they should leave, but they protested that all their effort would be wasted and people would think them unstable. Agathon replied that some might criticize them, while others might recognize that they were willing to leave even hard-won work for the sake of what mattered more.

The disciples were afraid of losing what they had built and of how their decision would appear to others. Agathon was less concerned with preserving the project than with preserving the purpose for which the project had been undertaken. He would not let past effort become a reason to remain somewhere that no longer seemed good. When he prepared to go, the disciples asked to leave with him.

### Devotional

There is a particular difficulty in walking away from something that has cost us greatly. Time, money, effort, reputation, and hope can become arguments for continuing long after we begin to suspect that continuing is unwise. The more we have invested, the harder it becomes to imagine letting go.

Agathon does not treat previous effort as meaningless. The cell was genuinely built, and the labor given to it was real. Yet yesterday’s investment could not decide what faithfulness required today.

We often remain in situations because leaving would seem to invalidate everything that came before. We tell ourselves that we have already come too far, worked too hard, or sacrificed too much to change direction now. Agathon shows that a past commitment does not have to become a permanent captivity.

There is also the fear of appearing inconsistent. The disciples imagine others watching them and concluding that they cannot stay committed to anything. Agathon accepts that people may interpret the same decision very differently.

Discernment therefore requires something deeper than avoiding criticism. There are times when endurance is faithful, and there are times when endurance becomes stubbornness. Wisdom lies partly in learning the difference.

This saying does not encourage us to abandon every difficult situation. Agathon himself was known for persistence, discipline, and long labor. What moves him here is not discomfort but the perception that remaining would harm the life they were trying to protect.

Sometimes letting go is itself an act of endurance. We endure the loss of sunk effort, the uncertainty of beginning again, and the possibility of being misunderstood. That can require more courage than simply staying with what is familiar.

Agathon invites us to hold even our hard-won achievements lightly. A home, project, role, plan, or identity can serve us for a season without becoming something we must preserve at any cost. When the deeper purpose asks us to move, we can honor what came before and still be free to leave.
