## Day 15 — Do Not Carry It Inside

### Abba John the Dwarf

Abba John once went to the church at Scetis and heard some of the brothers arguing with one another. When he returned to his cell, he did not go inside immediately. Instead, he walked around the cell three times.

Some brothers saw him and wondered what he was doing. John explained that his ears were still full of the argument and that he wanted to clear his mind before entering. He did not want to carry the noise of the conflict into the place where he prayed.

The argument had ended outside him, but something of it was still continuing within. John understood that peace sometimes requires a transition. Before entering the cell, he needed to let what he had absorbed begin to settle.

### Devotional

Some encounters end long before they leave us. The conversation may be over, the meeting finished, the message sent, and the other person already gone. Yet inwardly, we may still be standing in the same room.

We replay what was said and imagine what we should have answered. We rehearse better arguments, relive the tension, and continue carrying the emotional atmosphere long after the moment itself has passed. The body moves on, but the mind does not always follow.

John notices this even though the argument was not his. He had merely heard other people disputing, yet their agitation had entered him. The noise outside had become noise inside.

So he walks around the cell.

There is something wise in how ordinary this action is. John does not demand that his mind become peaceful instantly, and he does not shame himself for having been affected. He simply gives himself time and movement before crossing the threshold.

We may need similar thresholds in our own lives. Modern life often moves us from one emotional environment to another without allowing any space between them. We leave a difficult meeting and immediately enter another conversation, finish reading something upsetting and turn toward our family, or carry the tension of a commute straight through the front door.

The nervous system does not always change rooms as quickly as the body does. Something can remain activated after the event itself is over. Without noticing this, we may hand the residue of one experience to people who had nothing to do with it.

Perhaps we need to sit in the car for two minutes before going inside. Perhaps we need to walk around the block, breathe slowly, wash our hands, make tea, pray, or simply name what we are carrying. The practice itself matters less than the pause it creates.

Awareness opens a small space between what happened and what happens next. In that space we may realize, *I am still angry*, or *I am bringing someone else’s anxiety with me*, or *I am about to make another person carry the weight of a conversation they were never part of*. Naming what is present gives us a chance to release some of it before it spills outward.

John’s three circles become a form of mercy. They protect not only his own interior peace but also the people and spaces he is about to enter. He recognizes that what we fail to process often becomes something someone else has to absorb.

Stillness sometimes requires more than closing the door. Sometimes we first have to notice what we are bringing through it. Peace begins when we allow one moment to end before carrying its unfinished noise into the next.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me what I am carrying.*

**Breathing out:** *Let me leave it here.*
