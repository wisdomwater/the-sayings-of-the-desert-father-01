## Day 19 — Return to the Spring

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen remembered the words of the psalm: “As the deer longs for flowing streams, so my soul longs for you, O God.” He said that deer wandering in the desert may swallow poisonous creatures, and when the venom begins to burn within them, they desperately seek water. They drink deeply, and the cool water relieves what is burning inside.

Poemen saw the same pattern in the spiritual life. The monks sat in the desert and encountered thoughts, temptations, fears, and the bitterness of their own inner struggles. By the end of the week, they longed to gather again at the Eucharist, returning to Christ as thirsty animals return to the spring.

The water did not mean that the desert had never happened. It meant that what had entered them did not have to remain there forever. They could return to the source of life and be renewed.

### Devotional

There are experiences that leave something behind in us. A harsh conversation may continue burning long after the words are finished, or a disappointment may settle into resentment before we even notice it. Fear, envy, shame, frustration, and desire can all enter quietly and begin changing the interior atmosphere.

Poemen calls this venom.

The image is strong because venom does not simply disappear by pretending it is not there. Something has entered the system and is now moving through it. The deer knows only that it is burning and that somewhere there is water.

Much of the spiritual life may consist of learning where to go when something within us begins to burn. We often respond by trying to numb the feeling, distract ourselves, or discharge it onto someone else. We may reach for food, entertainment, work, argument, fantasy, or anything else capable of briefly making the discomfort quieter.

Poemen offers a different movement: return to the spring.

For the monks, this meant the Eucharist. After a week of struggle, they gathered again around the bread and cup and remembered that their lives were not sustained by struggle alone. They returned to Christ, not as people who had perfectly survived the desert, but as thirsty people who needed to drink again.

There is something deeply compassionate in this image. The monk is not condemned for having venom in him, because the desert itself contains things that bite and poison. The question is what he does once he recognizes the burning.

Perhaps we all need places of return.

For some, that may indeed be the Eucharistic table, where bread and wine become a repeated reminder that grace is received rather than achieved. It may also be prayer, silence, Scripture, friendship, confession, walking beneath trees, or sitting long enough for the nervous system to remember that it is safe to stop fighting.

What matters is that we know where the spring is.

Without such places, bitterness accumulates. We carry one wound into another conversation, one frustration into the next day, and one fear into every decision that follows. Eventually the poison begins to feel like our natural state.

But Poemen does not ask us to become immune to the desert. He assumes we will encounter things that wound, tempt, and disturb us. The wisdom lies in returning often enough that what enters us does not become what defines us.

There is humility in thirst. To thirst is to admit that we cannot generate everything we need from within ourselves. Something outside our own effort must sustain us.

Perhaps this is why the psalm does not say that the deer has mastered the desert. It simply longs for water. And perhaps contemplation is sometimes no more complicated than remembering, in the middle of whatever is burning, where the spring can still be found.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I thirst for living water.*

**Breathing out:** *Cool what burns within me.*
