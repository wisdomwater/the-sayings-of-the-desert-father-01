## Day 84 — Beyond Praise and Blame

### Abba Macarius the Great

A brother came to Abba Macarius asking for a word that might help him live faithfully. Macarius told him to go to the cemetery and insult the dead, and the brother did exactly as he was told. When he returned, Macarius asked whether the dead had answered him, and the brother said they had remained silent. The next day Macarius sent him back to praise them lavishly, and again they gave no response.

Macarius then asked the brother to notice what he had seen. The dead had not been disturbed by insult, and they had not been inflated by praise. He told the brother to become like them in this respect. Freedom would grow as neither contempt nor admiration was allowed to determine who he was.

### Devotional

Most of us are deeply responsive to how other people see us. A kind word can lift us for hours, while a cutting remark can stay with us for days. Macarius notices how easily the opinions of others can begin governing the inner life.

His image of becoming like the dead is deliberately severe. He is not asking us to become emotionally numb or indifferent to relationship. He is pointing toward a freedom in which every passing judgment does not gain authority over the self.

Praise can be as powerful as criticism. We may begin adjusting ourselves to preserve admiration, repeating what earns approval and hiding what might threaten it. Without noticing, the desire to be liked can become another form of dependence.

Criticism pulls from the opposite direction. A harsh word may awaken defensiveness, shame, anger, or the urgent need to prove ourselves. The more our identity depends on the verdict of others, the more power those verdicts acquire.

Macarius invites us to place both praise and blame in a larger frame. We can listen carefully when criticism contains truth and receive encouragement when praise is sincere. Neither needs to become the final measure of our worth or direction.

This kind of freedom makes genuine correction easier. When criticism no longer threatens our entire identity, we can examine it without collapsing or retaliating. We can ask what is true without accepting every accusation as a definition of who we are.

It also makes praise safer to receive. Gratitude can replace the need to cling to admiration, because a good word does not have to become something we continually chase. We can enjoy encouragement without building our lives around earning the next dose of it.

Macarius points toward an inner steadiness that does not require the world to keep approving us. Praise may come, and criticism may come, but neither has to possess the center. We can remain attentive, teachable, and grounded while letting both pass through without becoming our master.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Root me deeper than praise.*

**Breathing out:** *Free me from the fear of blame.*
