## Day 22 — I Do Not Know

### Abba Anthony

Some elders once came to visit Abba Anthony, and among them was Abba Joseph. Anthony wanted to test their understanding, so he brought forward a passage from Scripture and asked each person what it meant. One by one they offered their interpretations, and Anthony told each of them that they had not understood it.

At last he turned to Joseph and asked what he thought the passage meant. Joseph answered simply that he did not know. Anthony responded that Joseph had found the way precisely because he was willing to say so.

The wisdom in the room appeared not as a better explanation, but as freedom from the need to produce one. Joseph did not have to protect himself from uncertainty. He could remain truthful even when the truthful answer was incomplete.

### Devotional

There is a particular discomfort in not knowing. We often feel that an unanswered question exposes some deficiency in us, especially when other people are watching. The impulse to fill the silence can become stronger than the desire to be truthful.

So we offer an answer.

Sometimes it is well considered, and sometimes it is mostly a defense against appearing uncertain. We reach for something plausible, repeat something we have heard, or speak beyond what we actually understand. The words may sound confident even when the ground beneath them is thin.

Anthony sees something more trustworthy in Joseph’s restraint. Joseph does not confuse intelligence with the ability to produce an explanation on demand. He is willing to let the boundary of his knowledge remain visible.

That kind of humility can be surprisingly difficult. We build identities around being competent, informed, spiritually mature, or able to see what others have missed. Admitting uncertainty can feel less like describing the limits of our knowledge and more like threatening the person we believe ourselves to be.

But uncertainty does not make us smaller.

In fact, the ability to say *I do not know* may create the very space in which genuine learning can begin. If we rush too quickly to an answer, we stop listening. What we already think we know fills the room before anything deeper has a chance to appear.

This matters far beyond intellectual questions. We often want explanations for suffering, other people’s motives, the future, God, ourselves, and the strange turns our lives have taken. There are moments when wisdom gives us an answer, but there are also moments when the most faithful response is to remain beside the mystery without pretending it has been solved.

Religious language can make this especially difficult. A tradition gives us words for enormous realities, and those words can become precious vessels of meaning. Yet having a word for something is not the same as possessing the reality the word points toward.

We can say *grace*, *providence*, *salvation*, *God*, or *love* and still be standing before a mystery deeper than our explanation. Humility allows doctrine to remain meaningful without pretending that our concepts have enclosed what is infinite. It lets language point without mistaking the finger for the horizon.

There is also tenderness in admitting what we do not know about another person. We may see their behavior and immediately assign a motive, constructing a story that makes the situation easier to understand. Often the more humble response is simply to acknowledge that we do not know everything happening inside them.

That restraint creates room for compassion.

Joseph’s answer is not anti-intellectual. He does not reject study, interpretation, or the pursuit of understanding. He simply refuses to claim more than he has been given.

Perhaps this is one of humility’s quietest freedoms. We can learn without needing to appear learned, ask without embarrassment, revise our understanding, and stand before mystery without immediately covering it with words. We do not have to know everything in order to remain faithful to what we do know.

Sometimes the doorway into wisdom is not another answer.

Sometimes it is the courage to say, *I do not know.*

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Free me from false certainty.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to remain open.*
