## Day 96 — The Freedom of Being Small

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen taught that a person who learns to regard himself as small will find rest wherever he lives. His words were simple, yet they pointed toward a freedom that many people spend their lives seeking. Peace did not depend upon finding the perfect community, gaining the respect of others, or arranging circumstances according to one's preferences. It began with releasing the need to occupy an important place in the world.

For the desert monks, becoming small did not mean denying one's humanity or accepting mistreatment. It meant loosening the grip of pride, comparison, and the constant desire to establish one's significance. A person who no longer needed to be admired could live without continually defending his reputation. Wherever such a person went, he carried less of the burden that made ordinary life so difficult.

### Devotional

Much of our restlessness comes from the desire to know where we stand. We compare our accomplishments with those of others, wonder whether our contributions are appreciated, and become troubled when we feel overlooked. Even in relationships built upon affection, we may quietly measure how much attention or respect we receive. We want to know that our presence matters.

Poemen suggests that peace may come not from finally securing our importance, but from becoming less concerned with it. His teaching can initially sound like an invitation to diminish ourselves or abandon a healthy sense of worth. Yet there is a difference between believing that we are insignificant and no longer needing to prove that we are significant. The first produces shame, while the second can open a remarkable freedom.

Consider how much energy we spend managing the impressions others have of us. We explain our intentions when misunderstood, rehearse conversations in which we wish we had spoken more wisely, and quietly resent those who seem to receive recognition more easily. Sometimes clarification is necessary, especially when a misunderstanding causes genuine harm. But often our distress arises because the image others hold of us has become something we feel responsible for protecting.

The desert tradition recognized how easily the desire for recognition can follow us into even the most secluded places. A monk might abandon possessions and public honors only to become proud of his humility or severe discipline. The outward circumstances had changed, but the underlying hunger remained. Spiritual practice itself could become another way of establishing superiority.

Poemen offers a different understanding of freedom. If we no longer require every encounter to confirm our worth, we can begin meeting people without turning them into judges of our value. Their approval may bring encouragement, and their criticism may offer something worth considering, but neither determines who we are. We become more available to the relationship itself because we are less preoccupied with what it says about us.

This does not require us to become passive or indifferent to injustice. Humility does not mean allowing others to exploit us, nor does it ask us to silence our convictions when something important is at stake. We can establish boundaries, disagree honestly, and pursue meaningful work without making our personal importance the center of every concern. In fact, the less we need to defend our identity, the more freely we may be able to act from love.

There is also a profound tenderness in the idea of becoming small before God. If our worth arises from being loved rather than from demonstrating our importance, we have nothing essential to establish. We do not need to compete for a place in divine affection or distinguish ourselves from those around us. We may simply receive the gift of existence and allow others to receive it alongside us.

Poemen invites us to discover the rest that becomes possible when we relinquish the exhausting work of self-importance. We can participate fully in life, offer our gifts, and take responsibility for our choices without demanding that everything reflect favorably upon us. There is room to be ordinary, unfinished, and occasionally misunderstood. Perhaps we will discover that becoming small does not diminish our lives, but makes them spacious enough for love.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I release the need to prove my worth.*

**Breathing out:** *Let me rest in Your love.*
