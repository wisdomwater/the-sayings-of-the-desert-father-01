## Day 13 — You Do Not Have to Enter Everything

### Abba Arsenius

Abba Arsenius taught that when a monk traveled away from his cell, he should be careful not to become entangled in everything happening around him. He did not need to enter every conversation, involve himself in every dispute, or make every concern his own. By moving through the world without being pulled into every passing matter, he could preserve his inner peace.

Arsenius was not teaching indifference. He was teaching discernment about what deserves our attention and what does not. Peace depends, in part, on learning that not everything we encounter requires our participation.

### Devotional

Some forms of distraction are easy to recognize. We know when we are wasting time, scrolling without purpose, or avoiding something important. Other distractions are harder to notice because they arrive dressed as responsibility.

Something is happening, so we feel we must have an opinion. Someone is arguing, so we feel we must decide who is right. A controversy appears, and before long we are reading, reacting, discussing, and carrying a problem that may have very little to do with the life actually entrusted to us.

Arsenius offers a surprisingly freeing alternative. You do not have to enter everything. Not every argument needs your voice, and not every disturbance needs to become an interior burden.

This does not mean withdrawing from love or refusing responsibility. There are moments when love requires involvement, courage, advocacy, or presence. Someone may genuinely need our help, and peace that depends on ignoring suffering is not the peace Arsenius is describing.

But constant involvement is not the same thing as love. We can become entangled simply because something captured our attention, and once it has, we may keep feeding it long after our involvement has ceased to be useful. A conflict between strangers can occupy the heart just as thoroughly as one happening in our own home.

Modern life makes this kind of entanglement almost effortless. A small device in our pocket can present us with hundreds of events, arguments, injustices, opinions, and crises in a single day. Each one quietly suggests that awareness should become emotional participation.

Yet attention is not unlimited. Every concern we carry occupies some part of the interior world, and eventually that accumulation changes the atmosphere of the heart. We may discover that our peace has been spent on matters we were never actually called to hold.

Arsenius invites us to ask a simple question: *Is this mine to carry?* Sometimes the answer will be yes, and when it is, we should enter fully and faithfully. But sometimes the answer will be no.

In those moments, release is not negligence. We can care without taking possession, bless what we cannot carry, and allow a problem to remain outside the boundaries of our responsibility. Letting go can be an act of trust.

This kind of restraint may even make us more present to the part of the world that is ours to serve. When we stop trying to attend to everything, we may notice more clearly the person in front of us, the work before us, the relationship that needs care, or the suffering we can actually relieve. Attention becomes less scattered and more available.

Peace is not found by controlling everything outside us. It may begin when we stop allowing everything outside us to take up residence within. The contemplative heart learns not only how to pay attention, but also what to let pass.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me what is mine to carry.*

**Breathing out:** *Help me release the rest.*
