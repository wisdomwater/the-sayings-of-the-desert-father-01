# Day 50 — When Love Matters More Than Appearance

### Abba Sisoes

Abba Adelphius, the bishop of Nilopolis, came to visit Abba Sisoes on the mountain of Abba Anthony. Before the bishop and his companions began their journey home, Sisoes served them food early in the morning even though it was a fast day. While they were eating, some other brothers arrived tired from their travels, and Sisoes told his disciple to give them food as well.

Adelphius hesitated because he feared the newcomers would think Sisoes regularly ate early on fast days. Sisoes nevertheless told his disciple to feed them, and the visitors soon realized that the elder had broken his fast because guests were present. Adelphius then understood that he had been concerned with how the situation would appear, while Sisoes had been concerned with what love required.

### Devotional

There is a subtle difference between practicing a discipline and wanting others to know that we practice it. At first the two may seem almost identical, because the outward behavior can look exactly the same. The difference becomes visible when love asks us to do something that might make us appear less disciplined than we actually are.

Adelphius does not object because eating itself is wrong. He is concerned about what the arriving brothers might conclude when they see Sisoes eating early on a fast day. They do not know the circumstances, and they may assume the elder has become careless about a practice they respect. The bishop wants to protect Sisoes from being misunderstood.

That concern is understandable, yet Sisoes sees another priority. The travelers are tired, food is available, and they have arrived at his door. Their need matters more to him than preserving their impression of his ascetic discipline. He is willing to let his reputation become ambiguous if hospitality requires it.

This touches a very ordinary temptation in spiritual life. We may begin a practice because it helps us become more loving, attentive, or free, but eventually the practice becomes part of the identity we present to others. We become the person who fasts, serves, prays, eats a certain way, lives simply, or maintains a particular discipline. Once that identity becomes important, protecting the appearance of faithfulness can quietly compete with faithfulness itself.

Sisoes does not reject fasting. His willingness to break the fast on this occasion only makes sense because the practice actually matters to him. He simply refuses to make the discipline more important than the purpose toward which it points. The practice remains a servant rather than becoming a master.

This requires a kind of interior freedom. If our spiritual identity depends on appearing consistent, we may become anxious whenever circumstances require flexibility. We can begin making decisions based on how they will look rather than on what love actually asks of us. Sisoes is free enough to be misunderstood.

There is humility in that freedom. He does not need the arriving brothers to recognize the severity of his usual practice. He does not explain himself beforehand or make sure they understand that this meal is an exception. Their opinion of his discipline is simply not the most important thing in the room.

Hospitality often asks for this kind of flexibility. Another person may interrupt our schedule, disturb our silence, alter our plans, or require attention at an inconvenient moment. Sometimes love means maintaining a boundary, but sometimes it means allowing the boundary to bend because a human being is standing before us. Discernment lies in knowing which moment we are actually in.

This also gives us a way to examine our own practices. We can ask whether prayer is making us more available to people, whether fasting is deepening compassion, and whether simplicity is loosening our attachment to control. A discipline that consistently makes us harsher, more defensive, or more concerned with appearances may need to be reconsidered. The fruit helps reveal whether the practice is serving its purpose.

Sisoes closes this movement on love and hospitality with a quiet but demanding lesson. The goal is not to become impressive spiritual people whose lives display flawless consistency. The goal is to become people who are increasingly available to love, even when love disrupts the image we hoped to maintain. Sometimes the most faithful thing we can do is allow another person's need to matter more than being seen as faithful.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Free me from the need to appear holy.*

**Breathing out:** *Make me available to love.*
