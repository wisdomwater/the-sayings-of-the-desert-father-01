## Day 100 — The Freedom to Love Without Hatred

### Amma Syncletica

Amma Syncletica taught that it was good to avoid anger, but she also recognized that anger sometimes arises within us. When this happened, she urged her disciples to remember the apostle Paul's instruction not to allow the sun to set upon their anger. If a single day was too long to carry resentment, she wondered, why would anyone allow it to remain throughout an entire lifetime? Her concern was not merely with the feeling of anger, but with the way an unhealed wound could gradually become a settled disposition of the heart.

She then offered a striking distinction between the person who causes harm and the destructive forces that have taken hold of them. In the spiritual language of her time, she attributed the deeper source of wrongdoing to the devil rather than to the person alone. She urged her listeners to direct their opposition toward the sickness rather than the one who suffered from it. Her teaching invited them to recognize wrongdoing without allowing their response to become hatred toward another human being.

### Devotional

There are moments when another person's actions wound us so deeply that anger seems the only reasonable response. We may feel betrayed by someone we trusted, mistreated by someone in authority, or harmed by another person's carelessness. Our anger can arise as a natural recognition that something precious has been violated. Yet what begins as a response to harm can gradually become something that shapes the way we see the person who caused it.

Syncletica recognizes the danger of allowing anger to become a permanent dwelling place. She does not suggest that we can always prevent anger from arising, nor does she ask us to pretend that wrongdoing has not occurred. Instead, she draws our attention to what happens when anger remains unresolved. A feeling that initially alerts us to injustice can slowly harden into resentment, suspicion, and hatred.

There is an important difference between recognizing that someone has acted wrongly and concluding that the person is nothing more than their wrongdoing. When we have been hurt, the distinction can feel almost impossible to maintain. We remember the words that were spoken or the choices that caused suffering, and those memories begin to define the person in our minds. Eventually, we may find ourselves unable to imagine anything about them beyond the harm they have caused.

Syncletica invites us to see more deeply. Her language of sickness reflects an understanding that destructive behavior emerges from something disordered within the human heart. Fear, ignorance, woundedness, pride, and unrestrained desire can all lead people to act in ways that diminish themselves and others. Recognizing these influences does not excuse harmful behavior, but it may help us resist the temptation to reduce another person to their worst actions.

This distinction is especially important when we consider the difference between forgiveness and reconciliation. We may release our desire for another person's suffering without restoring a relationship that has become unsafe. We can recognize someone's humanity while maintaining firm boundaries or seeking justice for the harm they have caused. Love does not require us to surrender discernment, nor does compassion demand that we expose ourselves to repeated injury.

There is also a freedom that emerges when we no longer need hatred to validate our pain. Sometimes we fear that releasing resentment will mean minimizing what happened or allowing the person who hurt us to escape responsibility. Yet the truth of our suffering does not depend upon our continued anger. We can remember clearly, speak honestly, and pursue what is right without allowing another person's wrongdoing to determine the condition of our own hearts.

Perhaps this is one of the deepest expressions of divine love: the ability to oppose what destroys life without ceasing to desire the healing of those caught within it. Such love does not deny evil or pretend that every wound can be repaired easily. It holds together two convictions that we often struggle to reconcile: that harm matters profoundly, and that no human being should be reduced entirely to the harm they have done. Justice and mercy need not be enemies when both are directed toward the restoration of what has been broken.

As we conclude our journey through the wisdom of the Desert Fathers and Mothers, Syncletica leaves us with an invitation that reaches beyond the solitude of the desert. The practices of silence, humility, discipline, repentance, and prayer were never meant to become achievements we could admire in ourselves. They were ways of making room for a love less governed by fear, resentment, and the need to defend our own importance. Perhaps the deepest fruit of the spiritual life is not that we finally become perfect, but that we become increasingly free to love, even in a world where love remains difficult.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Let me see others through Your love.*

**Breathing out:** *Free my heart from hatred.*
