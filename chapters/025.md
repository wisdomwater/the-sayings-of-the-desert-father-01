## Day 25 — The Soil of Reverence

### Abba Euprepius

A brother once asked Abba Euprepius how reverence for God comes to dwell in the soul. Euprepius answered that if a person has humility and poverty, and if he refuses to judge others, then the fear of God will come to him. He described reverence not as something we force into existence, but as something that grows under certain conditions.

The soil matters. A heart made simpler by humility, less possessive by poverty, and less occupied with judging others becomes more available to God. Reverence appears where there is room for it.

### Devotional

The phrase *fear of God* can be difficult for modern ears. It can sound like anxiety before a dangerous deity, or the feeling that one misstep will bring punishment. But the desert tradition often means something closer to reverence: the deep awareness that we live before a Reality greater than ourselves.

Euprepius does not tell us to manufacture that feeling. He gives us three practices that make the heart receptive to it. Humility, poverty, and non-judgment slowly clear the interior space.

Humility loosens our need to be at the center. We no longer have to be the most important person in the room, the one who understands everything, or the one whose interpretation must prevail. The world begins to feel larger because it is no longer required to revolve around us.

Poverty carries a similar freedom. For the desert monks, this often meant literal simplicity of possessions, but the spiritual movement reaches further. We can become poor in our need to possess outcomes, status, certainty, admiration, or control.

This kind of poverty is not deprivation for its own sake. It is the discovery that we can survive without clutching everything we thought we needed to secure ourselves. The hands open, and something in the heart opens with them.

Then Euprepius adds a third condition: do not judge others.

Judgment can consume enormous amounts of interior space. We compare, evaluate, assign motives, rank people, and rehearse what is wrong with them. The mind becomes crowded with other people’s failures.

The irony is that this often leaves very little room for reverence. When we are continually positioning ourselves above or against someone else, the world becomes smaller and flatter. Everyone is reduced to a verdict.

Non-judgment does not mean losing discernment. We still recognize harm, make decisions, set boundaries, and name what is true. But we stop pretending that seeing one part of another person gives us possession of the whole.

That restraint changes the atmosphere of the heart.

Another person becomes mysterious again. Their history, wounds, motives, fears, and hidden acts of courage remain partly beyond us. We begin to remember that every person stands before God in ways we cannot see.

Perhaps reverence grows from that same recognition. Reality is larger than our categories, and God is larger still. The more we release the need to master, possess, and judge everything, the more space there is for wonder.

This is why Euprepius’s three practices belong together. Humility says, *I am not the center.* Poverty says, *I do not have to possess everything.* Non-judgment says, *I do not fully know another person.*

Together they make the soul spacious.

Reverence may not arrive as a dramatic religious feeling. It may appear quietly as tenderness, restraint, gratitude, or a new willingness to listen. The ordinary world begins to feel less ordinary because we are no longer rushing to reduce it to ourselves.

Perhaps the fear of God is not something we have to summon.

Perhaps it grows naturally when the heart becomes simple enough to notice that it is standing on holy ground.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Make my heart simple.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to stand in reverence.*
