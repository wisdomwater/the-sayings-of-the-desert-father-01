# Day 43 — Love That Stays

### Abba Agathon

While traveling into town to sell his wares, Abba Agathon came across a sick stranger lying in a public place with no one caring for him. Agathon rented a room and stayed there with the man, working with his own hands to pay the rent and using the rest of his earnings to meet the stranger’s needs. He remained with him for four months, until the man recovered.

Only then did Agathon return to his own cell. His compassion was not a passing impulse or a single gift given on the way to somewhere else. He allowed another person’s need to rearrange his life for as long as care was required.

### Devotional

Many acts of kindness are brief. We give money, bring a meal, send a message, offer a ride, or stop for a conversation before continuing with the rest of our day. These gestures matter, but Agathon shows us a form of love that asks for something more sustained.

He does not merely notice the sick traveler. He allows the encounter to interrupt his plans and alter the shape of the next several months. The stranger’s need becomes part of Agathon’s own daily life.

There is something demanding about this kind of love. It is easier to respond generously when the need has a clear ending. A single act can feel meaningful and complete, while long-term care becomes repetitive, inconvenient, and sometimes emotionally draining. Agathon remains present after the initial compassion would have faded.

His care is also deeply practical. He rents a place to stay, works to pay for it, and uses what remains to provide for the sick man. There is nothing dramatic in the description, only the steady accumulation of ordinary choices. Love becomes visible through rent, labor, food, and time.

This can challenge our tendency to separate spiritual life from logistical life. We may imagine compassion primarily as a feeling of concern or an inner posture of love. Yet another person’s suffering often requires schedules, money, transportation, phone calls, errands, meals, and repeated acts of attention. Agathon shows that practical details can become the very substance of mercy.

He also does not seem preoccupied with efficiency. Four months is a long time to remain with a stranger whose life had no obvious connection to his own. Agathon does not calculate whether the investment is proportionate or whether someone else might be more responsible. He simply recognizes that a person has been left alone and decides not to leave him there.

Most of us have limits that Agathon’s story does not fully address. We cannot take responsibility for every need we encounter, and wisdom requires attention to our own obligations, health, families, and capacities. Sustainable love sometimes requires sharing the burden rather than carrying it alone. The story is not a command to become responsible for everyone.

It does, however, ask whether we leave too quickly. There are times when we offer initial help but withdraw once the situation becomes inconvenient or repetitive. We may grow impatient when someone is not recovering fast enough, changing quickly enough, or becoming independent on our preferred schedule. Agathon stays until the healing has had time to unfold.

That patience is part of hospitality. Hospitality is more than opening a door for a moment. It creates enough room for another person to remain while they are still vulnerable. Sometimes what a wounded person needs most is the confidence that care will not disappear simply because recovery takes longer than expected.

There is also a quiet humility in Agathon’s story. He does not appear to make himself the hero of the sick man’s recovery. He works, provides, stays present, and then returns home when the man is well. His help is substantial without becoming possessive.

That may be one of the healthiest forms of care. We remain long enough to help without needing another person to remain dependent on us. We give what is needed while respecting that their life is still their own. Love stays through the vulnerable season and then knows how to release.

Agathon invites us to consider where love may be asking for endurance rather than intensity. The work may not be dramatic, and it may consist of many small acts repeated over weeks or months. Yet faithfulness often looks exactly like this: continuing to show up after the first moment of compassion has passed.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Give me patience to remain.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me love that stays.*
