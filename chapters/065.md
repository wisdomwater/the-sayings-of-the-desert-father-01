## Day 65 — The Work of Becoming

### Abba John the Dwarf

One of the fathers asked Abba John the Dwarf what a monk is. John answered with a single idea: toil. A monk labors in everything he does, giving himself patiently to the work before him. For John, the spiritual life is not something acquired quickly or possessed without effort.

His answer is simple enough to sound severe, but it is also realistic. Formation happens through repeated practice, attention, failure, and return. The work is rarely dramatic, and much of it may remain invisible to everyone else. What matters is the willingness to keep participating in the slow work of becoming.

### Devotional

We often prefer transformation that arrives suddenly. A powerful insight, decisive moment, or new beginning feels more satisfying than small efforts repeated over months and years. John brings us back to the ordinary persistence from which most change is actually made.

Toil does not necessarily mean exhaustion. It means accepting that worthwhile things often require sustained attention and that progress may be gradual. The labor is found in returning to what matters when novelty has disappeared.

This is true in prayer, relationships, work, and the shaping of character. Patience is formed by practicing patience in situations where impatience would be easier. Generosity grows through repeated acts of giving rather than through admiration for the idea of generosity.

Much of this work can feel unimpressive. We may repeat the same practice, confront the same weakness, or make the same small choice many times before noticing any change. The absence of dramatic results does not mean the labor is wasted.

John’s teaching also leaves room for failure. To labor at something is not the same as performing it perfectly. Real work includes mistakes, correction, rest, and beginning again.

There is a particular temptation to abandon a practice once its difficulty becomes familiar. At first, effort can feel meaningful because everything is new, but repetition eventually exposes impatience and boredom. Staying with the work then becomes part of the work itself.

Patient labor also protects us from judging ourselves by short stretches of success or failure. A good week does not mean we have arrived, and a difficult week does not erase everything that came before it. Formation is usually visible only when we look across a longer horizon.

John’s answer finally turns attention away from the image of the person we want to become and toward the next act of faithfulness available now. We do not have to complete the whole journey today. We only have to keep doing the work that belongs to this moment.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Give me patience for the work.*

**Breathing out:** *Teach me to remain faithful.*
