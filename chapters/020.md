## Day 20 — You Do Not Have to Look

### Amma Sarah

It was said of Amma Sarah that she lived beside a river for sixty years. Day after day the water flowed near her cell, always available to her sight. Yet during all those years, she never turned her gaze toward it simply because it was there.

Sarah's discipline can sound almost incomprehensible to us. The river was not evil, and there was nothing sinful about looking at water. Her practice seems instead to have been about discovering that the presence of something interesting does not create an obligation to attend to it.

### Devotional

There is an austerity in Sarah's story that may initially seem excessive. Why refuse to look at a river, especially something beautiful enough to offer relief in the severity of the desert? Surely contemplation should deepen our capacity to notice the world rather than teach us to ignore it.

But perhaps the river itself is not really the point. Sarah had chosen a life ordered around a particular intention, and the river represented something continually available to draw her attention elsewhere. By leaving it unseen, she was practicing the freedom to decide where her gaze would rest.

We live in an age that rarely asks us to exercise that freedom. Almost everything around us is designed to attract the eye, awaken curiosity, and create the feeling that something worth knowing may be happening just beyond our present attention. Another headline, message, video, argument, notification, or image is always waiting.

None of these things has to be bad in itself. That may be precisely why Sarah's river matters. The deepest distractions are not always obviously destructive things; sometimes they are simply interesting things that have learned how to summon us.

We can begin to live as though availability creates obligation. If a message arrives, we should read it, and if a link appears, we should follow it. If someone is arguing somewhere, perhaps we should know what they are arguing about, and if the world offers another small novelty, perhaps we should turn our head toward it.

Sarah quietly says no.

Her refusal is not contempt for the river but possession of her own attention. She has learned that noticing something and following it are different movements, and that the second does not have to follow the first. Something can exist beside us without becoming the center of our awareness.

There is tremendous freedom in that distinction. A thought may appear without requiring investigation, a desire may arise without requiring satisfaction, and an irritation may announce itself without receiving an hour of rehearsal. Even something beautiful or interesting can be allowed to remain where it is while we continue with what matters more.

Attention is one of the ways we give ourselves to the world. What we repeatedly look toward begins to fill our imagination, shape our desires, and establish the interior landscape in which we live. To choose our attention, then, is not merely to manage distraction; it is to participate in the formation of the heart.

This does not mean becoming closed to beauty or indifferent to what surrounds us. There are times when contemplation asks us to look more deeply at a tree, a face, a cloud, or the light moving across water. The question is whether we are truly looking or merely obeying the restless reflex that says every new thing deserves us.

Sarah lived beside the river without needing to possess it with her eyes. The water could flow without becoming another demand, and beauty could exist without being consumed. She had learned that the world does not disappear when we decline to attend to every part of it.

Perhaps this is one of the gifts of stillness. We gradually discover that attention can remain where we place it, even while countless other things pass nearby. We do not have to follow every movement at the edge of our vision.

The river can keep flowing.

And we can remain where we are.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Gather my attention.*

**Breathing out:** *Let the rest pass by.*
