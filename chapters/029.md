## Day 29 — As Necessary as Breath

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen said that a person needs humility and reverence for God as much as the breath that comes through the nostrils. He did not describe them as advanced virtues reserved for unusually holy people. He placed them among the most basic conditions of spiritual life.

Breathing is not something we accomplish once and then move beyond. It must continue quietly, moment after moment, usually without attracting our attention. Poemen suggests that humility and reverence must become just as continual.

### Devotional

We often imagine spiritual growth as accumulation. We learn more, pray more deeply, become more disciplined, and gradually acquire virtues that were once missing. Progress seems to mean adding something to ourselves.

Poemen gives us a different image.

Breathing adds nothing impressive to our identity. We do not wake in the morning proud that we successfully breathed through the night. Breath simply keeps us alive.

Perhaps humility is meant to work in the same way. It is not another achievement to display but the quiet movement that keeps every other virtue from becoming distorted. We breathe humility into prayer, work, relationships, failure, success, and everything else we do.

Without it, even goodness can become suffocating.

We begin protecting our image as a good person, a wise person, a competent person, or a spiritual person. We become anxious when something threatens that identity. The very virtues that once opened us toward God begin closing around the self.

Humility creates space again.

It lets us admit that we were mistaken without collapsing into shame. It allows another person to know something we do not, to possess gifts we lack, or to see something in us that we would rather not see. We inhale reality instead of continually trying to reshape it around ourselves.

Poemen joins humility with the fear of God, which we might understand as reverence. Humility remembers our size, while reverence remembers the vastness of what surrounds us. Together they restore proportion.

We are small, but we are not meaningless. We are limited, but we are held within something immeasurably larger than our limitations. Reverence allows that larger reality to remain larger.

This may be why Poemen compares these qualities to breathing rather than to some heroic act. We need them repeatedly because the ego continually closes in again. We forget, grasp, defend, compare, control, and then remember once more that we are creatures rather than the center of reality.

So we breathe again.

Humility may be as simple as noticing when we have begun tightening around ourselves. We loosen the grip, release the need to be right, and remember that our worth does not depend on controlling the room. The movement can happen many times in a single day.

Reverence is the corresponding outward breath. We remember God, mystery, the other person, the world beyond our own concerns. Attention expands beyond the narrow boundaries of the self.

There is no final breath after which breathing is complete. Likewise, there may be no point in the spiritual life when humility becomes unnecessary. The deeper we go, the more naturally we may breathe it, but the need remains.

Perhaps holiness is less about becoming spiritually impressive and more about learning to breathe freely before God. We receive life, release control, receive again, and release again. Humility and reverence become the rhythm by which the soul stays alive.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *I receive the life I am given.*

**Breathing out:** *I release the need to be greater.*
