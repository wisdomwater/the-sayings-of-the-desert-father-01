## Day 27 — The Lighter Burden

### Abba John the Dwarf

Abba John said that we have set aside the lighter burden of self-accusation and taken up the heavier burden of self-justification. His language is deliberately surprising because most of us experience the opposite. Admitting fault feels heavy, while explaining ourselves feels like relief.

John sees more deeply.

Self-justification may protect us for a moment, but it creates a burden we must keep carrying. Once we commit ourselves to proving that we were right, we have to defend the story again and again. Humility offers a lighter way: tell the truth about our own part and let the need to win begin to fall away.

### Devotional

The phrase *self-accusation* can sound harsh to modern ears. It may evoke shame, self-contempt, or the habit of assuming that everything is our fault. That is not the freedom John is describing.

He is pointing toward responsibility rather than condemnation.

When something goes wrong, the mind often begins building a case almost immediately. We remember what the other person said first, why our reaction was understandable, what circumstances made our behavior inevitable, and how anyone in our position would have done something similar. Some of those explanations may even be true.

But explanation can quietly become defense.

Before long, we are no longer trying to understand what happened. We are trying to establish innocence. Every new detail becomes another piece of evidence supporting the conclusion we already want.

That is the heavy burden John names.

Self-justification requires constant maintenance because reality is rarely as clean as our defense would like it to be. Perhaps the other person really did hurt us, but perhaps we also exaggerated, withdrew, retaliated, avoided a difficult truth, or said something meant to wound. Once we insist that we must be entirely right, those pieces become threatening.

Humility makes room for complexity.

We can say that someone harmed us and still ask what belonged to us. We can acknowledge unfair circumstances while noticing how we responded to them. Responsibility does not require pretending that everyone else was innocent.

This is why John calls the other burden lighter.

There is relief in saying, *This part was mine.* We no longer have to rearrange every fact to protect ourselves from it. The energy that went into defense becomes available for repentance, repair, learning, and change.

This does not mean accepting false blame. Some people have been trained to take responsibility for everything around them, including the choices and emotions of others. Humility is not agreeing with every accusation; it is becoming honest enough to distinguish what is ours from what is not.

Once that distinction is clear, we can carry our part without carrying the whole world.

Self-justification often arises because admitting wrong feels dangerous. We fear that one mistake will become a verdict on our character, as though saying *I acted selfishly* means *I am nothing but selfishness*. So we defend the action because we are actually trying to defend the self.

Grace allows another possibility.

If our worth does not depend on an unbroken record of being right, then repentance becomes much less threatening. We can look directly at failure because failure is no longer the final definition of who we are. Truth becomes something we can survive.

Perhaps that is what makes the burden light. We stop dragging around the exhausting task of proving ourselves innocent and simply stand before God as we are. What needs forgiveness can be forgiven, what needs repair can be repaired, and what needs changing can finally be seen clearly.

Self-justification keeps us tied to the past because the case must remain open. Humility allows the case to close. We acknowledge what is ours, receive mercy, and become free to take the next step.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me what is mine.*

**Breathing out:** *Free me from the need to justify myself.*
