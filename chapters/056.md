# Day 56 — The Wall of Self-Will

### Abba Poemen

Abba Poemen said that a person’s own will can become like a bronze wall standing between the soul and God. It can also become a stumbling stone, something that keeps us striking against the same obstacle again and again. When a person relinquishes that self-will, Poemen says the wall can finally be crossed.

But Poemen does not say that having a will is itself the problem. He adds that when our will becomes aligned with what is right, then we are able to labor well. The issue is not desire, intention, or agency, but the stubborn insistence that reality must conform to us.

### Devotional

Self-will is difficult to see because it often disguises itself as strength. We may tell ourselves that we are simply being decisive, principled, disciplined, or clear about what we want. Sometimes that is true, and sometimes we are refusing to release our demand that things happen according to our preferred script.

The image of a wall is useful because self-will can make us feel trapped while also being something we are helping to maintain. We push against circumstances, people, limitations, or outcomes that refuse to become what we expected. The harder we insist, the more solid the wall can seem.

This does not mean becoming passive. Poemen is not asking us to abandon judgment, responsibility, preference, or action. His final line makes clear that the will can be joined to what is right and become capable of real work. Surrender is not the destruction of agency but its reorientation.

Much of our suffering comes from the difference between what is and what we insist should be. We want another person to respond differently, the past to have unfolded differently, our bodies to behave differently, or the future to arrive according to plan. Some of those desires may be entirely reasonable. The trouble begins when peace becomes impossible unless reality first obeys us.

Self-will often speaks in the language of necessity. This must work, they must understand, I must succeed, this feeling must disappear, or life must become different before I can be at peace. The word *must* can quietly turn a preference into a demand. Once that happens, anything that resists us begins to feel like an enemy.

Letting go does not mean saying that everything is good. We can grieve what is painful, change what can be changed, and oppose what is harmful without requiring the world to surrender before we can remain grounded. Acceptance simply begins with seeing clearly what is actually here. From that place, action becomes less frantic and more truthful.

There is also a difference between desire and attachment to desire. We can want something deeply while leaving room for the possibility that it may not happen. We can pursue a goal without making our identity or peace depend on achieving it. The will remains alive, but it becomes less rigid.

Poemen’s wall may therefore be built from the refusal to bend. When we cling tightly enough to one outcome, one interpretation, or one way forward, we can lose our ability to notice what God may be doing outside our plan. Surrender creates space for a path we did not choose. Sometimes the wall falls because we finally stop demanding that the door appear where we expected it.

This requires trust. We may not know what lies beyond the relinquished expectation, and that uncertainty can feel vulnerable. Yet Poemen suggests that releasing self-will does not leave us empty. It allows our will to be joined to something larger and truer.

The goal is not to become a person who wants nothing. It is to become someone whose desires can remain open before God. We can act, choose, work, and hope without turning those movements into demands. Then the will becomes less like a wall and more like a path.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Loosen my grip.*

**Breathing out:** *Align my will with what is true.*
