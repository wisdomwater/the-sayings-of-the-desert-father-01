## Day 17 — A Sorrow That Cannot Be Distracted

### Abba Poemen

One day, while traveling through Egypt, Abba Poemen saw a woman sitting beside a tomb and weeping bitterly. He watched her sorrow and understood that, in that moment, nothing superficial could pull her away from what she was feeling. Even if every pleasure in the world had been placed before her, her heart would still have remained with the one she had lost.

Poemen said that the monk should carry something like this within. Not endless sadness, but a heart capable of being pierced by what is true and unwilling to be distracted from it too quickly. Her grief had gathered her entire attention into one place.

### Devotional

The desert tradition often speaks of *compunction*, a word that can sound foreign or severe to modern ears. It is sometimes translated as sorrow, repentance, or mourning, but none of those words quite captures its full meaning. Compunction is the piercing of the heart, the moment when something becomes real enough that we can no longer remain comfortably untouched by it.

Poemen sees this quality in the grieving woman. Her sorrow has simplified her, drawing her out of trivial concerns and into the immediacy of what has happened. She is not looking for entertainment, wondering what others think of her, or searching for something more interesting to occupy her attention.

She knows what matters because grief has made it impossible not to know. For that moment, the usual distractions have lost their power because something deeper has taken hold of her heart. Poemen is not praising grief for its own sake, but the undivided attention that grief sometimes creates.

This is important, because the desert teachers were deeply suspicious of our tendency to flee from uncomfortable truth. We may sense that something in our life needs to change and immediately reach for something that makes the discomfort easier to bear. We may recognize that we have wounded someone and quickly build an explanation that protects us from having to feel the full weight of it.

The same thing can happen with loneliness, fear, dissatisfaction, or longing. Before those feelings have had time to speak, we may fill the space with food, work, entertainment, conversation, scrolling, or noise. None of these things is necessarily wrong, but they can become ways of avoiding the quiet moment in which the heart is trying to tell us something.

Poemen's image invites us to remain a little longer. Instead of immediately escaping regret, we can allow it to teach us without turning it into shame. Instead of numbing grief, we can let it reveal what we loved and what still matters to us.

We can do the same with longing. Sometimes longing is not merely a problem to be eliminated, but a clue to what we have been seeking beneath the surface of our lives. Even the awareness of mortality can become clarifying when we allow it to remain with us long enough to ask what deserves the small number of days we have been given.

This kind of sorrow is not opposed to joy. In fact, it may protect joy from becoming shallow, because a heart that refuses grief must eventually refuse much of love as well. Love makes us vulnerable to loss, and becoming fully alive means allowing ourselves to be touched by reality in all of its beauty, pain, failure, mercy, death, and resurrection.

Perhaps this is what Poemen recognized in the woman beside the tomb. For one moment, she was completely present to what was real, and nothing superficial could draw her away. There is a strange holiness in that kind of undivided attention.

The invitation is not to become sad. It is to become difficult to distract from what is true, so that when something important reaches the heart, we do not immediately turn away. We stay long enough to listen, and perhaps long enough to be changed.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Let me remain with what is true.*

**Breathing out:** *Keep my heart awake.*
