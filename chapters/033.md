## Day 33 — Not Mine to Judge

### Abba Agathon

Whenever Abba Agathon noticed himself beginning to pass judgment on something he saw, he would speak to himself. He would remind his own mind that this was not his business. In doing so, he kept his spirit gathered and attentive.

The practice was small but continual. He did not wait until judgment had become a spoken accusation or an argument. He interrupted it while it was still forming inside him.

### Devotional

Judgment often begins very quietly. We see something, hear a remark, notice a decision, and almost immediately an interpretation appears. The mind moves from observation to conclusion with astonishing speed.

We rarely experience this as a choice.

It simply feels like seeing clearly. We tell ourselves that we are only noticing what is obvious, when in fact we may already be assigning motives, character, and meaning. A single action becomes a window through which we imagine we can see the whole person.

Agathon interrupts that process.

He does not argue with the thought or attempt to prove the other person innocent. He asks a more basic question: *Is this actually mine to judge?* That question creates space.

Much of what occupies our mind does not belong to us.

We carry opinions about how other people should spend their money, raise their children, manage their work, practice their faith, respond to conflict, or arrange their lives. Some of those matters may affect us directly, but many do not. We enter them mentally anyway.

This can feel strangely productive.

Judging gives the mind something to organize. It creates the comforting sensation that we understand what is happening and know what should be done. For a moment, complexity becomes simple.

But there is a cost.

Every judgment pulls attention away from our own life and places it inside someone else’s. We begin living imaginatively in decisions we did not make, motives we cannot know, and circumstances we only partially understand. The heart becomes scattered across a hundred situations that were never ours to carry.

Agathon calls himself back.

There is tenderness in that gesture. He does not condemn himself for having the thought, because thoughts arise. He simply refuses to follow it farther than it needs to go.

This does not mean that discernment is wrong. There are times when another person’s behavior affects us, when harm must be named, or when responsibility requires us to make a judgment about a situation. Wisdom does not mean pretending that everything is harmless.

The question is whether judgment is serving love or merely feeding itself.

We can recognize that something is unhealthy without needing to decide what kind of person someone is. We can set a boundary without constructing an inner prosecution. We can disagree without making another human being smaller in our imagination.

Agathon’s practice returns us to what is actually ours.

Our response is ours. Our speech is ours, our motives are ours, our next action is ours. The hidden interior life of another person remains largely beyond us.

There is freedom in accepting that limit.

We do not have to solve every person we encounter. We do not have to reach a verdict on every situation we witness. Some things can remain unfinished in our understanding.

Perhaps this is why Agathon’s spirit became recollected. Judgment scatters attention outward into lives we cannot control, while humility gathers it back into the place where transformation is possible. Again and again, he returned to the simple question: *What is mine to attend to now?*

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Gather my attention.*

**Breathing out:** *Release what is not mine to judge.*
