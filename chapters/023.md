## Day 23 — You Do Not Have to Defend Every Name

### Abba Agathon

Some monks once came to Abba Agathon because they had heard of his great discernment and wanted to test him. They began calling him things meant to provoke him: immoral, proud, and someone who spoke foolishly. To each accusation, Agathon calmly agreed.

Then they called him a heretic.

At that, Agathon refused the accusation. When they asked why he had accepted the earlier insults but rejected this one, he explained that the first charges were good for his soul to consider, while heresy meant separation from God. He had no desire to accept a name that implied such separation.

### Devotional

Most of us carry a collection of names we would very much like to protect. Competent, kind, intelligent, faithful, reasonable, generous, mature, good. When another person threatens one of those identities, something in us rises immediately to defend the border.

Agathon does something unsettling.

He allows the insults to pass through him without rushing to correct the record. When they call him proud or foolish, he does not insist that they recognize the better version of him. Instead, he seems willing to ask whether there might be something in the accusation worth examining.

This is not the same as believing every criticism we receive. People can misunderstand us, project onto us, speak cruelly, or simply be wrong. Humility does not require us to surrender reality to whoever speaks most harshly.

What Agathon releases is the need to control another person’s opinion of him.

That freedom is difficult because reputation feels closely connected to identity. If someone thinks badly of us, we may experience their judgment almost as an injury to the self. We replay conversations, compose imaginary defenses, and search for witnesses who will reassure us that the accusation was unfair.

Sometimes correction is necessary. A false accusation can harm another person, distort an important situation, or require a truthful response. Agathon himself demonstrates this when he refuses the charge that he has separated himself from God.

His humility therefore has boundaries.

He is not practicing self-erasure, and he is not saying that all labels are equally harmless. He distinguishes between insults that wound his pride and a claim that misrepresents the deepest orientation of his life. Humility does not mean becoming unable to say, *That is not true.*

The discernment lies in knowing what actually needs defending.

Much of what we protect may be the image of ourselves we want others to hold. We want to be seen as competent, spiritually mature, compassionate, insightful, or reasonable, and criticism threatens that carefully maintained portrait. The energy spent protecting it can become enormous.

Agathon seems strangely free from that burden. If someone calls him proud, he can let the word become a mirror rather than a weapon. Perhaps there is pride in him; perhaps there is not, but he does not need immediate vindication.

There is something powerful in being able to receive a criticism without either swallowing it whole or throwing it back. We can hold it quietly, examine what is true, release what is false, and refuse to let either praise or blame become our identity. The other person’s judgment then becomes information rather than a verdict.

This may be one of humility’s deepest freedoms. We no longer need every room to agree with our preferred description of ourselves. We can afford to be misunderstood sometimes because our life is not built entirely on being understood.

Agathon still knows what matters most to him. His relationship with God is not merely another reputation to manage, but the center from which the rest of his life takes meaning. Because that center is clear, many lesser accusations can simply pass by.

Perhaps humility is not learning to think badly of ourselves.

Perhaps it is learning which names no longer need defending.

### Breath Prayer

**Breathing in:** *Show me what is true.*

**Breathing out:** *Release me from the need to be defended.*
