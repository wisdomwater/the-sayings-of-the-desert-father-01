## Day 51 — Discipline Without Discernment

### Abba Anthony

Abba Anthony observed that some people had worn down their bodies through severe ascetic practices. Their discipline was real, and their effort could be seen in the way they lived. Yet Anthony said that because they lacked discernment, they remained far from God.

His warning was not against discipline itself. Anthony had spent his own life in fasting, solitude, prayer, and simplicity. What concerned him was the possibility of practicing all these things without learning how to recognize what actually leads the heart toward God.

### Devotional

Discipline can be powerful because it gives shape to desire. We choose a practice because we want something deeper than the immediate impulse of the moment. Over time, repetition can form habits that make us more attentive, free, and available to love.

Anthony reminds us that discipline alone cannot tell us whether we are moving in the right direction. We can become very skilled at denying ourselves while remaining harsh, proud, fearful, or preoccupied with control. The practice may grow stronger while the heart grows narrower. Discernment asks us to notice that possibility.

This is especially important because difficult practices can feel spiritually impressive. Sacrifice produces a sense of seriousness, and seriousness can easily be mistaken for depth. The more demanding the rule, the easier it may become to assume that keeping it proves we are becoming holy.

Anthony refuses that conclusion. He asks us to look beyond the effort itself and examine what the effort is producing. If a discipline makes us more patient, compassionate, honest, and awake to God, it may be doing its work. If it repeatedly produces pride, contempt, rigidity, or despair, something needs attention.

Discernment requires listening to more than the part of us that wants to succeed. We have to notice the motives beneath the practice and the fruit that emerges from it. Sometimes what looks like self-control is actually fear, and sometimes what looks like devotion is a need to feel superior or safe.

This does not mean abandoning every practice that becomes uncomfortable. Formation often involves resistance, frustration, and seasons when the fruit is not immediately visible. Discernment is not the same as choosing whatever feels easiest. It is learning to distinguish fruitful difficulty from needless harm.

There are times when discipline helps us resist desire, and there are other times when desire itself needs to be understood. A craving, fear, irritation, or restless thought may be carrying information about hunger, grief, loneliness, exhaustion, or unmet need. Simply suppressing the surface impulse may leave the deeper movement untouched.

Anthony’s warning invites curiosity about the inner life. Rather than asking only whether we obeyed the rule, we can ask what was happening within us as we kept or broke it. We can notice what we were seeking, what we were avoiding, and what kind of person the response was forming.

This is where humility becomes essential. We may discover that a practice we once considered unquestionably helpful is no longer serving us in the same way. We may also discover that the problem is not the practice itself but the way we have attached identity, fear, or self-judgment to it.

Discernment keeps the spiritual life responsive. It allows us to remain committed without becoming rigid and honest without becoming self-condemning. It remembers that the goal is not perfect control of the self but deeper communion with God.

Anthony’s saying is brief because the test is ultimately simple. Our disciplines are meant to lead us toward greater freedom, clarity, and love. When they become ends in themselves, we may become increasingly disciplined while quietly moving away from the very life we hoped to find.
